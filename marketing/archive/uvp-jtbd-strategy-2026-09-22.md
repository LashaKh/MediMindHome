## Step 1 — Customer types and complete JTBD map

The primary audience remains hospital decision-makers. The following are three useful buying situations, not mutually exclusive market segments. CFOs and IT leaders participate in the decision; they are not invented as separate customer markets.

### A. Independent hospital CEO or owner

Leads an established hospital and carries responsibility for care quality, financial performance, staffing, and major spending decisions. Needs a credible improvement path that can be defended to staff and owners.

Most important outcome: improve the hospital's clinical and business performance while keeping the transition manageable.

| Job | Desired progress | Trigger moment | Previous solution or workaround |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | Reduce wasted work and missed revenue, connect patient information, and modernize the hospital within a budget leadership can defend. | A budget review exposes weak margins; software renewal or an expensive change request forces a decision. | Separate programs, spreadsheets, manual reconciliation, and paying vendors for individual fixes. |
| Social | Be recognized as the leader who made a thoughtful improvement and protected the hospital. | The board asks for an improvement plan; staff challenge the current tools. | Preserve familiar systems, defer the decision, or choose a familiar vendor for reassurance. |
| Emotional | Feel confident that change will help and that there is a practical way to evaluate it. | A proposed replacement brings fears about disrupted care, staff resistance, or hidden costs. | Postpone replacement; require repeated demonstrations and references without an operational trial. |

### B. Hospital-chain CEO or group operator

Leads several hospitals and needs reliable visibility across them. Wants consistent ways of working without losing necessary local differences or making every improvement a separate project.

Most important outcome: run the network as a coordinated whole and spread successful improvements across its hospitals.

| Job | Desired progress | Trigger moment | Previous solution or workaround |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | Get comparable information, reduce duplicated work, and introduce improvements across multiple hospitals. | A new acquisition, branch expansion, or large performance difference exposes disconnected systems. | Local vendors, separate reporting teams, spreadsheets, and repeated implementation projects. |
| Social | Demonstrate that the group can grow while maintaining oversight and consistent care standards. | Owners ask why similar hospitals deliver different results or why expansion is difficult. | Demand more reports, add reporting layers, and appoint more coordinators. |
| Emotional | Feel in control of the whole network and confident that problems are visible. | A late report or cross-site incident shows that leadership learned about a problem too slowly. | Escalation calls, frequent meetings, and manual checking with each hospital. |

### C. Clinician-owner or medically led hospital

An owner or leader closely involved in clinical care sees how software affects staff attention and patient decisions. Wants the hospital to adopt AI in a way that supports good medicine and earns clinicians' trust.

Most important outcome: equip clinicians to deliver better care while making the hospital a credible early adopter of useful AI.

| Job | Desired progress | Trigger moment | Previous solution or workaround |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | Give teams a complete patient context, reduce paperwork, and turn clinical improvement ideas into tools they can use. | A handoff problem, missed information, excessive paperwork, or unanswered software request exposes a gap. | Staff compensate with phone calls, repeated checking, extra documentation, and separate AI tools. |
| Social | Be seen as a leader in modern care whom clinicians trust. | Colleagues or patients ask about AI; a rival announces a new capability. | Trial isolated tools or announce an innovation initiative without changing the shared foundation. |
| Emotional | Feel proud of the care environment and confident that technology supports clinical judgment. | Frustration that capable staff are held back by tools, or concern about AI acting without sufficient context. | Personally supervise exceptions, improvise workflows, or delay broader adoption. |

Cross-cutting emotional job: **Help me move the hospital forward without putting today's care, budget, or my reputation at risk.**

## Step 2 — Market sophistication

Working assessment: **4/5 — familiar promises require a concrete reason to believe.** This is an editorial assessment, not a measured market statistic. Familiarity will vary by hospital and region.

Current primary-source messaging supports the competitive premise:

- [InterSystems IntelliCare](https://www.intersystems.com/products/intellicare/) markets a unified system with integral AI and shared patient information.
- [Oracle Health EHR](https://www.oracle.com/health/clinical-suite/electronic-health-record/) markets an all-in-one system with AI embedded in workflows.

Therefore, whole-hospital software and built-in AI are useful category descriptions but do not establish uniqueness by themselves. MediMind's promising message combines the desired transformation with a concrete offer: a tailored parallel evaluation, matched current pricing for early partners, included continuing development, and a phased path toward proactive AI. This combination has not been proven globally unique.

Messaging implications:

1. Lead with the valuable change for the hospital and its people.
2. Explain the mechanism simply: replace disconnected programs with one shared system.
3. Make evaluation feel controllable: compare on the hospital's own workflows before switching.
4. Present matched pricing and included development as early-partner commercial terms.
5. Describe the proactive hospital brain as the destination being built toward, distinct from currently deployed workflows.
6. Use the opportunity to start early as urgency. Do not invent deadlines, customer disappearance, malpractice timelines, or guaranteed financial/clinical results.

The [Medplum case study](https://www.medplum.com/blog/medimind-case-study) is a useful public implementation reference. It does not establish guaranteed clinical outcomes, financial gains, or a universal two-week delivery guarantee. Current live footprint must be checked separately before publishing a deployment number.

## Step 3 — Ten UVPs as direct brand promises

### 1. Better care and an AI future within the current software budget

**Give your hospital better care today and an AI-run future—at the software price you already pay.**

Replace disconnected software with one system built to improve care, capture missed revenue, and reduce waste. Early partners get matched pricing and a clear path toward proactive AI.

Primary customer fit: A; also B and C.

### 2. Proof before the commitment

**See a better way to run your hospital before you commit to changing it.**

Test a tailored system beside yours in two weeks. Keep current operations running while your own team compares the work and decides whether to switch.

Primary customer fit: A; also B and C.

### 3. Continuous improvement without repeated development bills

**Keep improving your hospital without paying a new development bill for every idea.**

Unlimited future development is included. Your priorities shape the system as your hospital grows and its needs change.

Primary customer fit: C; also A and B.

### 4. More value from the care already delivered

**Get paid for more of the care you already deliver—and lose less to avoidable waste.**

Bring care and billing information together so your team can find missed charges, repeated work, and unnecessary spending.

Primary customer fit: A; also B. The desired financial improvement requires customer-specific measurement; no amount is established.

### 5. Time and attention returned to care

**Give your people more time for patients and less work fighting software.**

Connected information and AI assistance help reduce searching, repeated typing, and administrative work that competes with care.

Primary customer fit: C; also A and B.

### 6. A stronger basis for care decisions

**Give every care decision a more complete patient picture.**

Bring patient information together across the hospital, so teams and AI tools can work with relevant context instead of repeatedly rebuilding it.

Primary customer fit: C; also B and A.

### 7. A coordinated hospital network

**Run your hospitals as one coordinated network while preserving what each hospital needs.**

Give leadership a shared view and teams connected ways of working, with room to adapt the system to each hospital.

Primary customer fit: B. Secondary: A when expanding to multiple sites.

### 8. Improvement while the need is still urgent

**Get working improvements into your team's hands in days, while the need is still urgent.**

Requested features are ready to test in three business days. Your staff can evaluate the change and help shape what comes next.

Primary customer fit: C; also A and B. Testable work is distinct from validated clinical production deployment.

### 9. A hospital that anticipates and responds

**Move toward a hospital that sees problems coming and acts early, within the rules your teams set.**

Start with connected information and everyday AI. Build toward a hospital AI brain that anticipates risks and coordinates clinical and business decisions.

Primary customer fit: C; also B and A. This is a future direction, not a claim of a currently deployed hospital-wide brain or proven earlier detection.

### 10. A head start on attractive commercial terms

**Start building your hospital's AI advantage while early-partner pricing is still available.**

Begin improving today's work and preparing for tomorrow's care, with current software pricing matched and future development included.

Primary customer fit: B; also A and C. No fixed deadline or guaranteed advantage over competitors has been established.

## Step 4 — Scores

Scores are editorial judgments, not interview results or conversion forecasts. Each criterion uses a 1–5 scale. Clarity measures immediate understanding; credibility measures support in the reviewed evidence and realism of the commitment; desire measures fit with the emotional job. Credibility 3 means a plausible, relevant offer that needs demonstrated delivery; 2 means the important outcome or future capability is not yet established. No unsupported outcome receives a credibility score of 5.

| # | Proposition | Clarity | Credibility | Desire | Total / 15 |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | Better care + AI future + current price | 5 | 4 | 5 | 14 |
| 2 | See it before committing | 5 | 4 | 4 | 13 |
| 3 | Improvement without repeated development bills | 5 | 3 | 5 | 13 |
| 4 | More revenue captured, less waste | 5 | 2 | 5 | 12 |
| 5 | More time for patients | 5 | 3 | 4 | 12 |
| 6 | More complete patient context | 5 | 3 | 4 | 12 |
| 7 | Coordinated hospital network | 4 | 3 | 4 | 11 |
| 8 | Working improvements in days | 5 | 3 | 4 | 12 |
| 9 | Proactive hospital | 4 | 2 | 5 | 11 |
| 10 | Early AI advantage and partner pricing | 5 | 3 | 4 | 12 |

Why #1 leads: it joins ambition, present-day value, and the budget objection in one understandable promise. Its credibility is supported by the unified-system direction and founder-defined early offer, but measured outcomes and the full AI destination remain unproven.

Why #2 is strong: parallel evaluation answers fear of change with a tangible process. Internal deployment notes support the approach, while the two-week timeframe remains the founder's proposed offer.

Why #3 is strong: it sells an ongoing relationship in terms a buyer understands. The unlimited scope is compelling but should not be mistaken for proven unlimited delivery capacity.

## Step 5 — Top-three customer matches

| Top UVP | Best customer | Why it resonates | Cross-customer use |
|---|---|---|---|
| #1 | A: independent hospital CEO/owner | Lets the leader pursue a meaningful upgrade within a familiar software budget. | Broadest homepage message; relevant to B and C as well. |
| #2 | A: independent hospital CEO/owner; B during a cautious rollout | Gives leadership and staff a way to judge the system before making the switch. | Strong supporting message and call to action across all three. |
| #3 | C: clinician-owner or medically led hospital | Makes frontline ideas actionable without a fresh custom-development negotiation every time. | Also valuable to B for recurring network needs and A for financial predictability. |

Use #1 as the main promise, #3 as the distinctive ongoing value, and #2 as the reason to take the next step. These work together; they need not compete for headline space.

## Step 6 — Single-sentence hero statement

**We turn your current software budget into one connected hospital system built for better care, stronger finances, and an AI-run future.**

This combines the highest-scoring UVP with the emotional job of moving forward within controllable financial and operational commitments. The matched-price claim applies to the early-partner offer.
