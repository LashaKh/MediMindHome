# Prompt 1 — Describe Your Business & Market

> **Status and input (22 September 2026).** Working strategy for MediMind, a Georgian hospital operating system built on a shared FHIR R4 foundation. The first economic buyer is the CEO, owner, or group operator of a private multi-profile hospital or network; clinical, finance, operations, and IT leaders shape the decision. GeoHospitals is a founder-reported signed anchor agreement, not proof of a particular number of live sites or measured outcomes. Georgia is the first market; Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan are possible later markets. The founder describes the company as pre-revenue pending integration. Buyer pains below are hypotheses to test, not invented interview findings.

## Step 1 — Restate it back to me sharply

**MediMind gives private hospital leaders one operating system for clinical and business work, so they can test a clearer patient pathway, reduce repeated coordination, and gain a better basis for improving care and operations.**

That sentence deliberately names the buyer, the mechanism, and the change they can inspect. It does not claim an outcome has already been measured. The shortest version for a conversation is: “We help a hospital see and improve the work that one patient creates across departments.” The broad destination is a hospital whose information and operations work together, with AI assistance that staff review and a longer-term path toward more proactive coordination. That destination still needs proof one workflow at a time.

The product is a hospital operating system, not a single documentation tool or management dashboard. Its shared record matters because the same patient journey touches registration, orders, care, medicines, results, billing, and reporting. A buyer does not purchase the data architecture for its own sake. They buy a credible way to make those handoffs easier, with acceptable transition risk and cost.

## Step 2 — Name the real market I am in

The behavioral market is **hospital leaders already paying to compensate for work that fragments across clinical and business teams**. Their actual alternatives are doing more with the current supplier, asking staff to reconcile records, adding a focused tool, buying a broader established system, funding internal customization, or postponing replacement. This is the budget and management attention MediMind must win.

| What the hospital does now | Why it is rational | When a new operating system becomes relevant |
|---|---|---|
| Keeps its current hospital software and asks that vendor for changes | Familiar staff routines and known support avoid a disruptive decision | A recurring cross-department problem remains unresolved at an acceptable cost or speed |
| Adds coordinators, spreadsheets, phone calls, and manual checks | People handle exceptions flexibly | The same patient information is recreated so often that the work can be observed and measured |
| Adds reporting, integration, or a narrow AI product | Targets a specific pain while preserving the core | Another layer makes a task easier but leaves the underlying handoffs and data disagreement intact |
| Replaces the core with a major vendor or open-source implementation | Broad scope, references, or code access may reduce perceived risk | The actual proposal fails the hospital's local, commercial, adaptation, or transition requirements |
| Waits | Avoids immediate change to care and revenue processes | Leadership can name an ongoing cost or clinical workflow burden large enough to justify evaluating a change |

The practical first segment is not “every hospital in Georgia.” It is an organization with a named recurring pathway problem, an executive owner, a clinical or operations owner, an IT counterpart, and enough readiness to compare an agreed workflow. A 200–800-bed profile appears in internal buyer notes, but bed count alone is an unreliable qualifier. A multi-site group can be one buying organization with many facilities, while a single site can have a complex decision committee.

The first job is to learn what these buyers are trying to fix now. Internal notes suggest reimbursement gaps, avoidable staff effort, and delayed management visibility, but no local interview corpus establishes their frequency or ranking. At the point of sale, the relevant question is: **What was the last concrete patient pathway that forced people to switch screens, copy information, chase an answer, or reconcile a bill?**

## Step 3 — Surface my 3 biggest assumptions

1. **The pain is important enough to change the core.** A hospital can dislike its software and still prefer a small fix or the known incumbent. If independent buyers cannot point to a recent costly problem, an owner, and an evaluation window, whole-system replacement is a poor first ask. Test by comparing a scoped MediMind pathway proposal with the narrower remedy the hospital would otherwise buy.
2. **The shared system changes real work, not just the look of the screens.** Better data flow can plausibly reduce repeated entry or reveal gaps, but policy, payer contracts, staff habits, and clinical complexity also drive results. If a measured pathway takes no less time, creates new work elsewhere, or does not improve information reliability, the strategic premise weakens. Test task time, repeat entries, handoff completeness, error/rework, and actual collected revenue where appropriate; do not count an identified charge as cash received.
3. **The attractive offer can be delivered repeatedly at sustainable cost.** Matching current software price, including continuing development, presenting requests for test in three business days, and arranging a tailored parallel evaluation in two weeks are powerful only if scope, resources, and economics work at more than one organization. Test engineering effort, hospital staff effort, support load, infrastructure and AI usage costs, reuse of changes, and acceptance. A testable feature is not a production-approved clinical release.

Each assumption has a disconfirming observation. A buyer who praises a demo but will not supply a workflow owner has not validated demand. A faster screen without less total pathway work has not validated the benefit. One unusually cooperative anchor deployment has not validated the repeatable business model.

## Step 4 — Market sizing I can actually use

The defensible starting count is **232 hospitals and about 15,200 hospital beds in Georgia in 2025**, reported by [Geostat in July 2026](https://www.geostat.ge/en/single-news/3809/indicators-of-health-care-2025). This counts facilities, not purchasing organizations, qualified prospects, or buyers currently searching. It cannot be multiplied by a guessed annual contract value to produce a useful near-term market number.

Build the reachable market from identifiable organizations, one decision unit at a time:

| Filter | How to verify it | Count now |
|---|---|---:|
| Georgian hospital facilities | Official annual hospital statistics | 232 |
| Distinct organizations that buy hospital software | Deduplicate ownership and purchasing authority, including chains | Unknown |
| Private, multi-profile organizations whose operational complexity fits the product | Review actual service mix and workflow ownership | Unknown |
| Reachable through a lawful, named route to a decision-maker | Record introduction, contact route, and last verification date | Unknown |
| Actively seeking a solution | Record recent trigger, alternatives, budget route, owner, and timeframe from a real buyer | Unknown |
| Able to evaluate during MediMind's available delivery window | Confirm access, counterpart staff, data readiness, scope, and delivery capacity | Unknown |

**Direct answer:** A verified count of reachable businesses actively looking for a solution is unavailable. The answer is not 232 and not zero. Treat the number as a research output, not a slide assumption.

A useful first pass is a **15-organization research list**, including the anchor separately, and **8–10 conversations across at least four independent organizations**. These are proposed discovery targets, not an estimate of live demand. Publicly findable candidates in the prior research include Georgia Healthcare Group/Georgia Capital's healthcare operations, Aversi Clinic, and Vivo Medical Group; their existence does not imply interest or access. GeoHospitals belongs in an anchor/reference column, not the new-customer pipeline. A historical HelsiCore mention needs reconfirmation before it is treated as an active opportunity.

For each organization record the purchasing unit, facility count, named buyer and evaluators, current remedy, specific observed problem, trigger date, urgency, contract window, willingness to allocate an evaluation owner, and the next verified action. Only then calculate a bottom-up annual opportunity: sum the quoted recurring fees of eligible organizations, show implementation and optional AI separately, and attach explicit timing and conversion assumptions. A signed network agreement must not be inflated into a count of independently won facilities.

## Step 5 — Identify the key players

Official vendor descriptions establish the alternatives a buyer can hear about; they do not prove Georgian availability, local price, head-to-head performance, or a vendor's weakness at a particular site. “Fast-growing” cannot honestly be assigned without a growth dataset, so the map groups established and emerging alternatives by the job they compete for.

| Player or option | What it does well or offers | Limitation to test, not assume | Why a buyer may choose it now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current supplier, including [MEDSOFT/MEDWORK](https://medsoft.ge/en/about-us/) where installed | Local familiarity; the vendor describes hospital and government-system functions | Can the installed version solve this hospital's chosen pathway problem at acceptable speed and cost? | No migration, familiar support, and known work patterns |
| [InterSystems IntelliCare](https://www.intersystems.com/products/intellicare/) | Markets a broad clinical, administrative, financial, and AI-connected proposition | Compare local availability, implementation responsibility, adaptation, and total cost in an actual proposal | Breadth, vendor resources, and category credibility |
| [Epic](https://www.epic.com/software/health-systems-and-clinics/) | Broad clinical and enterprise scope with an established ecosystem | Check fit, localization, procurement path, and total cost for this buyer | Established references and institutional familiarity |
| [Oracle Health](https://www.oracle.com/health/clinical-suite/electronic-health-record/) | Broad EHR and embedded-AI direction | Distinguish available functionality from roadmap and check local fit | Familiar supplier scale or an existing relationship |
| [Bahmni](https://www.bahmni.org/) and implementation partners | Open-source hospital-system model and room for adaptation | Who pays for localization, hosting, maintenance, security, and support? | Greater code control or a trusted implementation partner |
| [Commure](https://www.commure.com/) | Focused clinical documentation and revenue-cycle automation | Does a targeted layer solve the recurring cross-department problem? | A narrower change can be easier to approve |
| [Qventus](https://www.qventus.com/solutions/healthcare-automation-platform/) | Operational automation, including actions in existing systems | Its scope and local fit must be evaluated against the specific pathway | The hospital may improve operations without replacing the core |
| Staff, spreadsheets, integration projects, and delay | Familiar, immediately controllable, often reversible | What is the continuing cost and residual error? | Avoids an uncertain vendor and transition |

The closest message-level challenge is not whether another vendor says “one system” or “AI”: several do. The competitive test is whether MediMind can show a hospital-specific pathway, a credible local implementation, clear ongoing adaptation, and a safe decision process at a cost the buyer can defend. There is no evidence here to claim that all incumbents are disconnected, cannot use AI, or are categorically slower.

The prompt asks for “fast-growing startups,” but a growth label needs revenue, customer, or deployment evidence that this research did not establish. Commure and Qventus represent newer adjacent options competing for attention and budget; the table describes their publicly stated scope without asserting a growth rate. This is a more useful buying comparison than an unsupported startup ranking.

## Step 6 — Pinpoint the gap

The underserved buyer is a private hospital leader whose clinical and business teams share a patient but still spend effort stitching that patient's work together, and who cannot justify a risky core replacement on a generic demo alone. That buyer needs a **visible, measurable first improvement** plus a way to understand the complete transition, not another abstract claim that software will “transform care.”

MediMind's proposed response has five parts. First, take an actual pathway across departments as the unit of change. Second, use a shared patient and operational foundation so information can follow the work. Third, let staff see the proposed pathway in a tailored parallel evaluation before a cutover decision. Fourth, offer continued development around the hospital's priorities. Fifth, reduce the initial subscription objection through the founder's early-partner price match. The exact coverage, total transition cost, request boundaries, and ongoing commercial terms must be made explicit per proposal; none is assumed to be zero or unlimited in delivery capacity.

This is a **gap hypothesis**, not a claim that no other company can deliver it. Its defensibility would come from repeatable local implementation, reusable care and business pathways, trusted support, and verified improvements. FHIR R4 is the shared technical base, like common grammar between departments; the hospital buys the easier conversation, not the grammar itself.

## Step 7 — Market positioning statement

**MediMind is the hospital operating system for Georgian private hospitals and networks that need clinical and business work to move together. Built on a shared FHIR R4 foundation, it lets a hospital start with one costly patient pathway, see how work and information can connect across teams, and evaluate a tailored version beside its existing system before deciding on a wider change. The early-partner offer matches current software pricing and includes continuing development, while the long-term product path adds more useful AI under the hospital's clinical control. The reason to act now is practical: repeated work and delayed visibility continue until the hospital measures and fixes a real pathway.**

The investor-deck version should append actual evaluation results when available, rather than presenting an intended benefit as achieved. “Why now” should be anchored to the prospect's recent event—contract renewal, cross-site expansion, an unresolved billing handoff, or staff time lost to a pathway—not to a fabricated market deadline.
