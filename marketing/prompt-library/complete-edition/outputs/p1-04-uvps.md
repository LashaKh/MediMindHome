# Prompt 4 — Generate Your UVPs & Map Your JTBD

> **Status and required inputs (22 September 2026).** This is Prompt 4 of the complete B11 sequence. Prompt 1 defines MediMind as a hospital operating system for Georgian private hospitals and groups, competing first with current software and workarounds. Prompt 2 maps discovery, a founder-led pathway demo, a readiness-dependent parallel evaluation, evidence review, and staged rollout; the first value moment is the hospital's own team seeing one handoff work with less reconstruction. Prompt 3 inventories all 25 canonical domains and finds the strongest BFG combination in a connected patient pathway plus buyer-controlled parallel comparison. These are completed working answers, not validated buyer research. The ten UVPs and scores below are editorial options, not observed conversion results. The three buyer types are executive buying situations; finance, clinical, operations, and IT leaders remain essential members of each committee.

### Required customer-type inputs

| Buyer type | Description in 2–3 sentences | Single most important outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Private multi-profile hospital CEO or owner** | Runs a hospital where clinical quality, staff capacity, and financial performance collide in one operating decision. May be close to daily management but must defend a major software change to clinicians, managers, and owners. | Improve one costly cross-department pathway with a transition and total cost the organization can justify. |
| **B. Private hospital-group CEO or operator** | Oversees more than one facility and needs a reliable view of comparable work without erasing necessary local differences. A new site, inconsistent numbers, or repeated implementation effort makes the limits of separate systems visible. | Establish a repeatable way to improve and oversee patient pathways across the network. |
| **C. Clinician-owner or medically led hospital executive** | Has purchasing influence and sees how software interrupts care and creates duplicate clinical work. Wants useful AI and connected context while preserving clinician judgment and staff trust. | Give clinicians a more coherent patient journey without asking them to accept unreviewed clinical automation. |

## Step 1 — Map the full JTBD for each customer type

The jobs below are **hypotheses drawn from internal positioning and hospital-context research**, not verbatim statements from interviews. “Job” means the progress a buyer is trying to make when an event forces a decision.

### A. Private multi-profile hospital CEO or owner

| Layer | Job | Trigger moment | Previous solution or workaround |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | Identify a pathway that wastes staff effort, obscures legitimate revenue, or delays decisions; fix it without destabilizing the hospital. | A contract renewal, repeated billing rework, staff overtime, or a report with irreconcilable numbers. | Ask the current vendor for a change, add checks and coordinators, buy another tool, or defer replacement. |
| Social | Be seen by owners, doctors, and managers as the leader who improved operations and protected care. | A board or clinical leadership meeting asks why the known problem persists. | Present another improvement plan or select a familiar vendor for reassurance. |
| Emotional | Feel confident that the change is worth the money and will not become a public operational failure. | The proposed replacement feels larger and riskier than the known problem. | Demand more presentations and references, then postpone a decision. |

The CEO's job is not to “adopt AI.” It is to deliver a better-run hospital with evidence and control. Price matching may open the conversation, but a visible pathway and credible transition make it a decision.

### B. Private hospital-group CEO or operator

| Layer | Job | Trigger moment | Previous solution or workaround |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | See comparable performance and spread an improvement across sites without rebuilding it from scratch each time. | A new acquisition, branch opening, unexplained variation, or a late group report. | Separate site suppliers, spreadsheet consolidation, local process owners, and repeated integration projects. |
| Social | Show owners and site leaders that growth comes with oversight and a practical standard of care. | A board asks why similar facilities cannot report or operate consistently. | Mandate more reporting and meetings while each site keeps its own workflow. |
| Emotional | Feel in command of the network while still respecting site realities. | A cross-site issue becomes visible only after its cost or clinical impact grows. | Rely on calls to local managers and after-the-fact reconciliation. |

For this buyer, one pathway is the prototype for a repeatable operating pattern. The proof must show both what can be standardized and what must remain local; a shared dashboard alone is not enough.

### C. Clinician-owner or medically led hospital executive

| Layer | Job | Trigger moment | Previous solution or workaround |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | Give care teams the relevant patient context and reduce documentation and handoff work while keeping clinical decisions with clinicians. | A missed result, a slow handoff, a near miss, clinician complaints, or another request the current system cannot accommodate. | Phone calls, duplicate notes, manual checks, separate tools, and personal oversight of exceptions. |
| Social | Be seen by clinicians and patients as a leader of safe, useful modernization. | Clinicians resist a new system or ask whether AI is being introduced responsibly. | Trial isolated AI tools or announce an innovation initiative without changing the underlying work. |
| Emotional | Feel proud of a care environment where technology supports staff rather than second-guesses them. | A clinician describes losing attention to screens or an AI proposal sounds autonomous. | Delay rollout or restrict it to low-risk pilots with little effect on the wider pathway. |

This buyer wants the path from “interesting technology” to “my staff trust using it.” The message must keep review rights and clinical governance visible. A claimed clinical outcome needs its own study, beyond a process demo.

**Shared core job:** “Help me improve how this hospital handles a patient, with enough evidence and control to defend the change.” It carries a functional outcome (less reconstruction and more reliable information), a social outcome (responsible leadership), and an emotional outcome (confidence rather than anxiety about a switch).

## Step 2 — Confirm market sophistication level

**Working score: Level 4 of 5, “New Mechanism.”** The label is a copywriting diagnosis, not a measured Georgian-market statistic. Established vendors such as [InterSystems IntelliCare](https://www.intersystems.com/products/intellicare/) and [Oracle Health](https://www.oracle.com/health/clinical-suite/electronic-health-record/) already describe broad connected systems with AI. Familiar claims like “one record,” “better care,” and “less admin” can establish relevance but cannot carry a distinctive message alone. Individual buyers who have endured failed replacements may react more like Level 5 (“Experience”), so the copy should invite firsthand evaluation early.

The right language shows a concrete mechanism and a controlled next step: **one patient → too many repeated handoffs → map the hospital's version → compare a connected path beside current work → decide from evidence.** Use the operating-system category, then plain language about the work people do. The FHIR R4 foundation can substantiate why data can share a structure, but should not replace the buyer's problem in the headline. The offer—matched current software price for eligible early partners, continuing development, three-business-day test target, and a readiness-dependent two-week parallel evaluation—supports the mechanism. These terms need written definitions. AI remains assistance under clinician review, with broader coordination a product direction.

## Step 3 — Generate 10 UVPs as brand promises

Every line below speaks directly to the buyer about a valuable change. Wording that says “can” or “see whether” is intentional where performance has not yet been measured. Some lines suit a specific pathway or buying situation better than a universal homepage headline.

1. **See where one patient's journey creates repeat work—and test a more connected path with your own team.** This is the broad promise and the clearest link to the campaign idea.
2. **Judge a tailored hospital pathway beside your current system before you decide to switch.** This answers the most serious buying fear with the founder's proposed parallel evaluation.
3. **Give your teams one clearer patient story to work from across care, coordination, and business tasks.** This is the core connected-context promise, subject to available source data.
4. **Put your next hospital improvement into your team's hands to test while the need is still urgent.** This draws on the three-business-day request target; it does not promise clinical production in three days.
5. **Keep improving the system with your hospital instead of negotiating a separate development project for every need.** This expresses included continuing development; capacity and request scope require written terms.
6. **Start improving hospital work without first increasing the software price you already pay.** This speaks to eligible early partners under the founder's price-match offer, not total cost or a lifetime price lock.
7. **See the same agreed operating measures across your hospitals, with room for each site's work to differ.** This is the group-operator promise; data definitions and site access must be verified.
8. **Help clinicians spend less effort reconstructing the patient story before they make a decision.** This is a process hypothesis, not a claim of improved clinical outcomes.
9. **Follow legitimate care work into billing and claim follow-up so your team can find what needs correction.** This avoids claiming that every identified gap becomes collected revenue.
10. **Use AI to help your people prepare and review work while your clinicians keep the final clinical decision.** This promises responsible assistance rather than autonomous care.

None should be presented as a customer quotation or a proven result. Each becomes stronger with a demonstrated workflow, a baseline, and a named person who reviewed it.

## Step 4 — Score every UVP

Each score is an editorial judgment from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). Clarity asks whether a CEO understands it on first read. Credibility asks whether existing product scope and the founder offer can support the *wording as written*, given missing outcome proof. Desire asks whether it speaks to the buyer's practical and emotional job. These are not survey scores or conversion predictions; small tests may change the ranking.

| # | Short name | Clarity | Credibility | Desire | Total / 15 | Why the score lands there |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | Find and test repeat work | 5 | 4 | 5 | **14** | Specific, inspectable, and relevant across roles; reduction remains to be demonstrated. |
| 2 | Parallel judgment before switch | 5 | 4 | 5 | **14** | Concrete founder offer addresses risk; readiness and scope need definitions. |
| 3 | One clearer patient story | 5 | 4 | 4 | **13** | Strong product fit, but completeness varies with source data. |
| 4 | Fast test of improvement | 4 | 3 | 4 | 11 | Attractive; request size, prerequisites, and test versus production must be explained. |
| 5 | Included continued development | 4 | 3 | 4 | 11 | Differentiates the relationship, but boundaries and capacity are unproven. |
| 6 | Early-partner price match | 5 | 4 | 4 | **13** | Founder-stated offer, easy to understand; does not settle total cost. |
| 7 | Comparable group measures | 4 | 3 | 4 | 11 | Valuable for groups; implementation and data definitions are site-specific. |
| 8 | Less reconstruction for clinicians | 5 | 3 | 5 | **13** | Highly desired; needs real task comparison before outcome wording becomes stronger. |

This tie-break is strategic judgment, not statistical superiority.

## Step 6 — Write the hero statement

**See how one patient pathway can run with less repeat work across your hospital, then decide from a parallel comparison whether to switch.**

This one-sentence hero combines the highest-scoring #1 promise with the buyer's central job: improve work while keeping the decision under hospital control. It is a recommended test headline, not a verified conversion winner. A more question-led campaign expression is **“Why does one patient create so much extra work?”** followed by **“Map my hospital's first improvement.”** In either version, the founder-led demo must deliver a concrete map, and the evaluation must measure the pathway before claiming a reduction.
