# Prompt 5 — Map Your Competition & Find Your Defensible Position

> **Status and inputs, 22 September 2026.** Working competitive strategy, not a verified win/loss report. Inputs are the completed Prompt 1 business and market answer; the Prompt 4 working jobs to be done (JTBD: the progress a buyer seeks) and UVPs; and current official vendor pages. The buyer is a Georgian private hospital CEO, owner, or group operator, with clinical, finance, operations, and IT leaders in the decision. The working top promises are a connected hospital foundation, a scoped parallel comparison before a switch, and continued development; their ranking remains a strategy judgment. No traceable prospect list of products considered, actual incumbent installations, local competitor proposals, or head-to-head prices was supplied. The prior market-sophistication estimate of 4/5 is editorial, not measured.

## Step 1 — Full competitive landscape

The first contest is for the decision to change at all. A hospital may know its software well, have staff who compensate for its limits, and regard replacement as more dangerous than the problem. Product pages establish what a supplier offers publicly; they do not establish a Georgian buyer's actual experience, quote, availability, or implementation quality.

Strong means the public offer or internal scope gives a credible reason to consider the option on that dimension. Neutral includes major uncertainty and is not a claim of average performance. Weak means a known structural mismatch or evidence gap for this decision. Validate every row against an actual hospital's installed version and proposal.

MediMind's **clear weakness** is independent proof and supplier continuity. Its breadth is not yet a scored victory over an accepted, operating hospital installation. It could also lose on transition burden once staff time, data migration, training, support, and parallel work are counted. A matched subscription price is not a matched total cost. No competitor is rated weak simply because its price or Georgian presence has not been verified.

## Step 3 — Find the white space

The most promising opening is **a Georgian private hospital with a named, costly pathway that runs across clinical and business teams, where the current supplier or a narrow add-on has not offered a satisfactory fix and leadership will only consider a core change after seeing a controlled local comparison**. MEDWORK already has local scope; IntelliCare, Epic, and Oracle have breadth and AI; narrow tools can improve a workflow with less disruption. MediMind could win through the *combination* of a local shared foundation, a hospital-specific first pathway, a readiness-dependent parallel evaluation, and a continuing development relationship at clear early-partner terms. The table does **not** prove no competitor can be strong on that combination. The whitespace is a testable buying situation and delivery practice, not a legally or globally exclusive product feature.

The proof artifact should be a one-page comparison for the hospital's chosen workflow: current steps, people and systems involved, time/rework baseline, proposed MediMind steps, staff and IT effort, costs, acceptance criteria, and rollback/cutover conditions. If a focused vendor can meet the same criteria with less risk, that is useful evidence against a replacement sale.

## Step 4 — Write and stress-test the defensible positioning statement

**Positioning to use:** “For Georgian private hospitals ready to fix a recurring patient pathway across departments, MediMind is a hospital operating system that connects clinical and business work on a shared foundation. We start with the workflow your team names, let you compare an agreed version beside current operations when prerequisites are met, and develop the system around what your hospital needs to improve.”

The prompt asks for an “only” sentence. A sentence such as “Unlike MEDWORK, IntelliCare, and Epic, MediMind is the **only** hospital operating system that unifies care and business work” fails immediately: their own pages describe overlapping scope [V1–V3]. “Only system with AI” fails against IntelliCare, Epic, and Oracle [V2–V4]. “Only system that adapts” fails against configurable and open-source alternatives. Even “only Georgian system with a parallel evaluation” is unsupported without exhaustive local contract research. A defensible version keeps the contrast **specific to an actual proposal**: “Unlike a renewal that leaves *this documented pathway* unresolved, MediMind offers to compare an agreed replacement workflow beside your current operation before a switch decision.” That claim must be supported by the buyer's own renewal and our actual evaluation agreement.

Stress tests against the top three alternatives:

1. **MEDWORK/current supplier:** It already describes local hospital modules. If it can fix the pathway promptly, with trusted support and lower transition cost, it may win. Ask for the incumbent's concrete plan, not a category argument.
2. **IntelliCare:** It already combines broad hospital work with AI. MediMind must show local fit, a better scoped evaluation, and sustainable terms in an actual comparison. Country availability and pricing are unanswered.
3. **Epic/Oracle-class enterprise suite:** A buyer may prefer established scale and references. MediMind can only counter with an accepted local workflow, transparent economics, a tested transition plan, and evidence of support. A smaller vendor is not automatically faster or cheaper.

The real defense, if built, is accumulated local implementation knowledge, reusable pathways, credible delivery, customer-approved reference evidence, and measured first improvements. FHIR R4 is a useful foundation, not a moat by itself.

## Step 5 — Identify my biggest competitive threat

**The incumbent supplier plus postponement** is the strongest next-12-month threat hypothesis. It has the data, staff habits, contract, and a built-in argument that change might interrupt care. It is also the least likely to appear in a startup competitor slide. This is a judgment, not a measured loss ranking.

Three actions now:

1. **Collect five recent pathway incidents in discovery.** Ask for the task, who touched it, the actual workaround, and what the incumbent has already been asked to change. Qualify out cases where the existing vendor has a satisfactory plan.
2. **Create one scoped comparison offer.** Agree on a workflow, synthetic or authorized data, readiness prerequisites, staff/IT effort, baseline, success criterion, cost, and a stop/cutover decision. Separate a two-week *evaluation target* from production sign-off.
3. **Build a reference and continuity pack.** With permission, show accepted module scope and one measured process result; document support ownership, backup and export, change control, and handover. Until then, state the proof gap. This directly addresses the rational fear of a young supplier.

## Step 6 — Build five competitive talking points

1. **When the buyer names MEDWORK or a local incumbent:** “That system may already fit your Georgian billing and reporting needs, and your team knows it. Which one patient pathway still needs manual repair? Let's compare your supplier's proposed change with a MediMind version, including the effort to switch.”
2. **When the buyer names InterSystems IntelliCare:** “IntelliCare presents a broad, AI-enabled hospital suite. For your hospital, I'd compare the actual Georgian module offer and implementation plan with one locally configured pathway we can put in front of your team. Then you can judge the work, price, and risk.”
3. **When the buyer names Epic or Oracle Health:** “Their breadth and supplier resources are real strengths. We should test the question that matters here: what can your own staff evaluate on the workflow you care about, under a plan they can safely accept? We can show our scope and gaps next to their proposal.”
4. **When the buyer names Bahmni/open source:** “Code access and flexibility are valuable. The practical choice is who will keep the system localized, secure, and supported over time. We can compare total responsibility, not just license fees, against the MediMind delivery agreement.”
5. **When the buyer names Commure, Qventus, or an AI add-on:** “A focused tool may be the least disruptive answer to that one task. If the same patient data still gets recreated between clinical, operational, and billing steps, let's measure the whole pathway and see whether improving the shared core is worth the larger change.”
