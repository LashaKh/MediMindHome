# Prompt 6 — Mine Competitor Reviews & Extract Customer Intelligence

> **Status and inputs, 22 September 2026.** This is a small, inspectable public-review sample, not a representative survey or Georgian buyer research. Prompt 5 maps the competitors; the working UVPs are a connected hospital foundation, a controlled first-pathway evaluation, and continued development; the buyer's job is to improve care and operating work without an uncontrolled switch. No founder-supplied review collection existed. Eleven first-person public reviews were inspected below, mostly hospital staff using Epic or Oracle's legacy Cerner-branded ambulatory suite. They are users, not verified economic buyers. Public reviewer roles and dates are preserved. Some Capterra entries are marked incentivized. No MediMind customer reviews or interviews have been invented.

## Step 2 — Extract real problems across four buckets

**What customers love.** Shared records and accessible patient context (R1, R3–R6, R8), plus flexible charting (R10). Established systems already deliver value; replacement risks losing it.

**What customers hate.** Task navigation or repeat entry (R1, R2, R9, R10); learning (R1, R3); site performance (R5); support/login (R7); coding data (R11). Causes and present-day product versions differ.

**What customers wish existed.** These are **inferred needs**, not explicit feature requests: less task friction (R1, R2, R9, R10), quicker support (R7), stable performance (R5), and correct local data (R11). R6 sought breadth and customization and moved **to** Epic.

**Why customers leave.** Only R6 states a switch rationale, and it is a switch **to Epic**. The inspected set provides no repeated reason for leaving Epic or Oracle. The requested “single most common reason” is therefore **unknown**. An unresolved current-vendor request remains a discovery hypothesis, not a review finding.

## Step 4 — Map problems to my product

Here **fully** would mean an accepted MediMind workflow and a buyer-verified outcome for the same task. We do not yet have that evidence in this edition. “Partially” means the product design or founder offer gives a plausible mechanism worth testing, not that the issue has been resolved for every user.

| Review-observed need/problem | MediMind fit now | What to show or measure before a stronger claim |
|---|---|---|
| Shared patient context across teams (R1, R4, R5, R8) | **Partial**. A FHIR R4 shared foundation and relevant modules exist in product descriptions; accepted scope varies. | Observe the same patient handoff and verify authorized information is complete, current, and usable. |
| Repeated charting across a selected pathway (R10) | **Partial**. A common record can reduce recreation, but only if the actual fields and workflow are designed and adopted. | Count entries and staff minutes before/after; catch new work elsewhere. |
| Too many steps for scheduling or chart review (R1, R2, R9) | **Partial**. Can redesign a chosen flow; no universal fewer-click proof. | Record screen actions, completion time, errors, and training time in the buyer's workflow. |
| Steep learning and rarely used features hard to find (R1, R3, R4) | **Partial**. A tailored path could improve fit, but replacement introduces its own learning burden. | Usability test with new and experienced staff, including training and help needs. |
| Slow support (R7) | **Unproven / not yet solved**. Included continuing development is not a verified support SLA. | Actual support ownership, response and resolution terms, and reference performance. |
| Crashes, freezes, and document timeout (R5) | **Not established as solved**. No comparative uptime or performance evidence. | Performance and availability tests under the hospital's load; incident process. |

**Marketing lead candidate:** the concrete problem of repeated work along a patient pathway, framed as a question and paired with an invitation to measure it. The product fit is plausible, not verified as a fully solved review problem. Accordingly, the next messages are *research-ready drafts*, not validated claims of superiority.

## Step 5 — Build three acquisition messages

Each opening repeats an exact review fragment. The rest is original, conditional copy. Before public use, test whether a Georgian buyer recognizes the underlying problem; do not represent the fragment as coming from that buyer.

1. **“too many clicks”** — If one patient journey sends your team through extra screens and handoffs, MediMind can map that journey across clinical and business work and show a scoped version beside your current system for your team to compare.
2. **“a lot of button pressing”** — If scheduling or another recurring task consumes staff attention, MediMind can build the chosen pathway into a shared hospital workflow and let your staff test whether the total work actually falls.
3. **“chart the same thing multiple times”** — If clinical facts are re-entered across departments, MediMind can connect the chosen steps on a shared record and measure repeat entries with your clinicians before any switch decision.

The exact phrases are from the cited Epic and older Cerner reviews, not from Georgian CEOs. None promises a measured benefit before the hospital performs the comparison. The third message should be used only if duplicate charting exists in the prospect's actual workflow.

## Step 6 — One-page review intelligence summary brief

**What the inspected voices actually say:** Broad systems can make records available and coordinated; staff still report task-specific navigation, learning, support, and technical problems. Positive reviewers often mention a pain in the same review. A switch to Epic for breadth/customization appears in one entry. The sample does not tell us why buyers leave a competitor, and it does not measure Georgian hospital demand.

**Five unmet-need hypotheses to test, not a prevalence ranking:** (1) less duplicate charting on one pathway; (2) fewer steps for common scheduling/chart review tasks; (3) timely, dependable support for a blocking task; (4) easy access to relevant patient context without losing breadth; (5) local coding, terminology, and workflow changes that stay correct after updates. The first two are closest to a MediMind pathway evaluation; the last three also create high proof obligations.

**Exact language:** The short fragments in Step 3 and the message openings in Step 5 come from favorable Epic reviewers and an older Cerner-era nurse review. They are small prompts for research, not a mandate to use English U.S. phrasing on a Georgian landing page. The encouraging language in Step 3 describes value competitors already deliver.

**Single most common reason to switch:** **Not determined.** Only one explicit switch rationale in this set was visible, and it was a switch *to* Epic for breadth/customization. A future win/loss log must capture recent triggers, prior vendor response, alternatives, and who approved the change.

**Three emotional triggers to investigate:** (a) frustration that a simple task takes repeated steps; (b) anxiety when the team cannot rely on current information or system performance; (c) relief and confidence when records are accessible and care-team work stays coordinated. These are interpretations of reviews, not a measured CEO emotion ranking.

**Next evidence move:** In buyer interviews, ask for the last specific incident, then observe one staff workflow. Ask whether the current supplier has already tried to fix it. Only after that should these provisional messages become public acquisition copy.
