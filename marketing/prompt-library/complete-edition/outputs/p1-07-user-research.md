# Prompt 7 — Design & Execute Your User Research

> **Status and inputs, 22 September 2026.** The **design** is complete below; execution has **not** occurred. No interviews, messages, community posts, observations, or buyer-provided records were collected for this prompt. Founder/internal notes describe a signed GeoHospitals relationship and historical access to a possible next organization, but do not supply independent buyer transcripts or permission to contact anyone. The research audience is Georgian private multi-profile hospitals and networks. The strategy to test is whether a CEO-owned patient pathway problem merits a core-system evaluation instead of an incumbent change request or narrow tool. The eight steps specify work to run, not findings claimed as already known.

## Step 1 — Audit my assumptions

Ranked by how much a contrary answer would change the product, offer, or market approach:

| Rank | Dangerous assumption | Why it could break the plan | Disconfirming observation to seek |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cross-department repeat work is painful enough to justify considering core replacement.** | The product may be solving a real inconvenience with an oversized purchase and migration. | Several independent CEOs can cite irritation but not a recent costly case, accountable owner, spending path, or reason a narrow remedy fails. |
| 2 | **The shared system improves total pathway work and information reliability.** | A prettier interface could simply move labor to another person or create double work during transition. | Observed tasks show no net time/error improvement after training, or missing data remains the main cause. |
| 3 | **A controlled parallel evaluation is feasible and changes the decision.** | If data, staff capacity, integrations, or governance prevent a test, the primary objection-remover fails. | Buyers who like the idea cannot authorize even a safe scoped comparison, or the trial requires more effort than the hospital can supply. |
| 4 | **The founder's attractive offer can be scoped and delivered sustainably beyond the anchor relationship.** | Matched current software pricing, continuing development, a three-business-day test target, and a two-week evaluation target may consume more capacity than the business can fund. | Independent cases require extensive one-off work or demand unbounded promises, and buyer value does not cover delivery cost. |
| 5 | **The CEO/owner is the right primary entry point and can mobilize the buying committee.** | A persuasive executive conversation might not move clinical, finance, IT, or procurement decisions. | Initiative starts elsewhere; a CEO cannot assign an owner, or clinical/IT leaders reject the path on non-negotiable requirements. |

Review data [P6] makes usability and support worth asking about, but does not validate any of these assumptions for Georgian CEOs. The research should deliberately seek cases where the incumbent works well.

## Step 2 — Define my research goal

**Goal:** Discover whether independent Georgian private-hospital leaders have a recent, important cross-department patient-workflow problem that their current remedy has failed to resolve, and determine what evidence, decision authority, and transition conditions would make them evaluate a shared hospital operating system. This is **generative** research (discovering how buyers understand and address a problem) with a small **evaluative** component (testing the proposed one-pathway comparison after the story is told). After the sprint, choose one first pathway and a better-defined buyer/offer, or narrow the product message if a focused change consistently wins.

The interview is not a product validation vote. A prospect saying the idea sounds good is weaker evidence than showing a real incident, naming the current cost and alternative, and committing an appropriate workflow owner to a scoped next step.

## Step 3 — Segment my user groups

| Group | Who; why they are valuable | Unique insight | Initial conversation target and saturation rule |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Economic sponsor** | CEOs, owners, or group operators of private multi-profile hospitals/networks. They own tradeoffs between care, cost, and transition risk. | What made this issue board-level, what alternatives are funded, who can authorize a test, and what would block purchase. | **4 conversations**, at least 3 independent buying organizations. This is a discovery starting point, not guaranteed saturation. Continue until new interviews stop changing the trigger/decision map, then test a different segment. |
| **B. Operating/economic workflow owner** | Finance/revenue-cycle or operations leaders at hospitals in the target profile. | What actually happens between care, billing, scheduling, inventory, and management reporting; what is measurable and controllable. | **3 conversations** across at least 2 organizations, preferably including a distinct independent site. Seek both a finance and an operations view if possible. |
| **C. Safety and delivery gatekeepers** | Clinical lead plus CIO/IT lead or their delegated workflow owners. This is one research group for access planning but two different perspectives to record separately. | Where patient safety, data quality, training, integration, support, and cutover could fail; which task staff would accept testing. | **3–4 conversations** across at least 2 organizations, including both clinical and IT voices. Do not call saturation if only one function has spoken. |

Initial total: **10–11 conversations** if access permits, across **at least four independent buying organizations**. That is an operational target, not a sample-size proof. Include no more than two anchor-network conversations in the first pass so one unusually cooperative relationship does not dominate. If recruiting falls short, record the shortfall and do not relabel internal conversations as independent validation.

## Step 4 — Build the interview guide

Target: 30–35 minutes, conducted in the participant's preferred language. With permission, review an anonymized workflow artifact or observe a safe task; no patient identifiers are copied into marketing notes. Ask for stories before concepts. Record exact quotations only with permission and context.

**Opening (2–3 minutes).** “We are learning how hospitals handle work that crosses teams, especially where a patient's information or a bill has to move between people. This is research, not a sales demo. I am interested in what actually happened, including when your current software worked well. We will not publish your name or organization from this conversation without separate permission. Is it all right to take notes? If we wanted to record, we would ask separately.” Confirm role, organization type, confidentiality expectations, and available time.

**Warm-up (5 minutes).** “What decisions are you responsible for?” “Which departments most often need to coordinate around a patient?” “What software and manual work sit in that path today?” “What has changed in the last six months?” Do not ask for a list of hypothetical wishes yet.

**Core (20–23 minutes; seven questions in this order).**

1. “Tell me about the most recent patient, operational, or billing handoff that took more work than it should. What happened, step by step?”
2. “Who noticed first, who had to repair it, and what was the consequence? What evidence exists without exposing patient details?”
3. “How often does that kind of incident happen, and what is your basis for that estimate?”
4. “What have you already asked your current supplier or internal team to change? What happened?”
5. “If you did nothing for six months, what would change for staff, patients, finances, or your own responsibilities?”
6. “What other remedy have you considered—training, a dashboard, integration, focused AI, new core software—and why did it win or lose?”
7. “If a team proposed a limited parallel comparison on that pathway, what would it need to show, who would need to approve it, and what would stop it?”

Only after those answers, show a neutral 30-second description of MediMind and ask: “What part fits, what part sounds implausible, and what would you need to inspect?” Avoid leading with AI or the price match, which can anchor the story. If the interviewee is not the economic buyer, adapt the last question to their approval role.

**Closing (3–5 minutes).** Summarize back the incident and decision route; invite corrections. Ask for one non-sensitive process artifact, a safe observation opportunity, or the name/role of the person who sees a different part of the same handoff. Ask whether a brief findings summary would be useful. Do not ask for a purchase commitment in a research interview; a later commercial discussion needs its own invitation.

## Step 5 — Build a documentation system

Use one folder per organization and one note per conversation, with an organization code rather than patient information. A simple spreadsheet is enough. Fields: date/language, role and site, recruiter relationship, consent/recording status, exact incident, observed versus reported facts, systems and people in order, current workaround, consequence and estimate basis, incumbent response, alternatives, buying trigger, decision participants, evaluation prerequisites, objections, direct quotation with permission flag, and the next evidence request. Keep a link to any authorized artifact, with access limits; do not duplicate sensitive material into the marketing workspace.

Within 24 hours, write a five-line debrief: the participant's job, the latest incident, the strongest evidence, the strongest counterevidence, and what this changes about the initial thesis. Tag statements as **observed**, **buyer-reported**, **team-reported**, **review-derived**, or **interpretation**. Keep a separate assumptions log with a confidence level and what would change it. Do not let a founder recollection become a verbatim buyer quote.

At the end of weeks 1 and 2, make a matrix of rows = organizations and columns = pathway, consequence, incumbent remedy, trigger, buyer authority, evaluation readiness, clinical/IT objections. Look for repeated patterns **across independent organizations**, not just repeated people within one network. Note negative cases explicitly. Summarize exact counts as `n/N` with role and source, not percentages or “most hospitals” from ten interviews. Saturation is assessed by whether new conversations still alter the decision map, not by reaching a fixed number.

## Step 6 — Research action plan

**Two-week target:** 10–11 completed conversations as above, at least four independent buying organizations, plus one permissioned observation or de-identified pathway artifact if feasible. This is a proposed sprint, not scheduled interviews. The founder should allocate interview time separately from the shared five-hour-per-week marketing-production budget; the ten conversations themselves, preparation, and synthesis require extra founder time.

**Day 1–2:** Ask Lasha to identify the actual GeoHospitals economic sponsor and a clinical/operations/IT counterpart he can approach appropriately. Record the signed relationship and accepted product scope separately. Ask whether the historical HelsiCore contact remains active; do not count it until confirmed. Build a deduplicated list of 15 buying organizations from public sources and legitimate introductions, including Georgia Healthcare Group, Aversi Clinic, and Vivo Medical Group only as research candidates, not presumed prospects.

**Day 3–5:** Start with one economic sponsor because a recent trigger and decision path determine whether any observed workflow is commercially relevant. Then interview the owner of that exact pathway. Contact independent organizations in parallel, using the tailored drafts in Step 8 only after the founder selects and authorizes recipients. Log refusals and non-responses without inferring lack of pain.

**Week 2:** Complete clinical and IT counter-interviews, observe an authorized task, and ask remaining CEOs whether the emerging problem statement matches a recent incident. Compare a narrow fix, current-supplier change, and a MediMind parallel evaluation on the same criteria. Create one one-page opportunity brief for each organization, then a cross-organization synthesis.

**Success at the end:** One sharply defined pathway to demonstrate, or a documented reason to change direction; at least four independently described recent incidents or clear negative cases; a map of who can authorize evaluation; known proof and safety requirements; and a decision on which claim remains hypothetical. A conversation count alone is not success. If access prevents the target, deliver the evidence gaps and revised recruitment plan rather than fictional themes.

## Step 7 — Tailored question sets for all three groups

These five per group supplement the common guide; select the relevant questions rather than extending every interview beyond its timebox.

**A. CEO/owner/group operator**

1. “At the last management meeting, which patient-workflow or operating issue required the most explanation, and why?”
2. “What has the current supplier been asked to improve, who approved that request, and what happened?”
3. “What cost or risk would make you fund a core-system change instead of another local fix?”
4. “Who could veto a parallel evaluation, and what would each person need to see?”
5. “If the evaluation worked, what decision would you be able to make in the next 6–12 months—and what budget or contract event governs it?”

**B. Finance/operations workflow owner**

1. “Walk me through the last case where clinical work, a charge, and a report did not line up. Where was the mismatch found?”
2. “Which manual reconciliations happen every day or week, and how do you count their time and corrections?”
3. “What portion of a payment delay or cost variance is software-related versus contract, policy, documentation, or staffing?”
4. “What did the existing supplier or an integration project change; what remained?”
5. “Which process measure and real financial measure could you safely compare in a two-week evaluation, and what data would be off-limits?”

**C. Clinical/IT safety and delivery gatekeeper**

1. “Where in the last patient handoff did staff leave the main record, re-enter data, or call someone—and what safety checks did that workaround provide?”
2. “What information must be complete and current for clinicians to trust a parallel workflow?”
3. “Which interfaces, migration steps, access permissions, backup tests, and audit records are mandatory before any real-data evaluation?”
4. “How would you detect a harmful change in documentation, medication, results, or care-team communication?”
5. “What training, support, stop rule, and clinical sign-off would you require before expanding beyond the first pathway?”
