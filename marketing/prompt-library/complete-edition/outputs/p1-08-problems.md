# Prompt 8 — Define the Real Problems You Are Solving

> **Status and inputs, 23 September 2026.** This is a decision-ready problem hypothesis, not a claim of completed Georgian buyer research. Prompt 1 defines MediMind and its market; Prompt 5 maps alternatives; Prompt 6 supplies a small public-review corpus from largely non-Georgian staff; Prompt 7 is a research plan with **no completed interviews**. The current hero expresses better care, connected work, and an AI future but does not contain a single exact problem sentence supplied by customers. Internal wording describes disconnected programs and repeat work; it is not a buyer quotation. The chosen first audience is a private-hospital CEO/owner or group operator. Clinical, finance, operations, and IT leaders must still validate the problem and the remedy.

## Step 1 — Separate real from assumed problems

**Evidence key.** “Validated” here would mean traceable evidence from the target buyer or an observed target workflow, plus enough context to know its cause and importance. “Partially validated” means a real but indirect observation exists. “Assumed” means a strategic interpretation or unmeasured effect. No problem is fully validated for the target Georgian economic buyer in the supplied record.

| Claimed or tempting problem | Status and source | What the evidence does and does not say |
|---|---|---|
| Staff perform repeated entry and too many steps on some EHR tasks. | **Partially validated** as a problem experienced by external software users [R1, R2, R9, R10]. | First-person staff reviews describe task friction. They do not establish frequency, cost, cause, or existence at Georgian prospects. |
| Teams value shared patient context and still struggle to find some information. | **Partially validated** as an external-user need [R1, R4, R5, R8]. | The same reviews praise competitor breadth; a “no existing system connects work” claim would contradict them. |
| Existing supplier changes are too slow or expensive for Georgian private hospitals. | **Assumed** for prospects; internal positioning raises it [I1]. | No independent vendor-change timeline, proposal, or buyer quote was supplied. |
| Clinical, financial, and operational handoffs cause material revenue leakage. | **Assumed** as a local causal/size claim; internal economic-buyer thesis [I2]. | Payment contracts, policy, documentation quality, and staffing may dominate; detected missing charges are not realized collections. |
| Leaders cannot obtain timely, trusted network-wide performance information. | **Assumed**, internal buyer thesis [I1, I2]. | No management report, reconciliation time, or executive testimony inspected. |
| A connected system can reduce total staff work and improve decisions. | **Assumed outcome**; plausible mechanism and product scope. | No before/after observation or accepted first-pathway result here. Training and parallel work may offset gains. |
| Buyers want a core replacement and will permit a parallel evaluation. | **Assumed demand**, founder offer and strategy [I3]. | A controlled comparison sounds useful, but no independent buyer acceptance or readiness record was supplied. |
| Better patient outcomes, higher revenue, and guaranteed compliance result from the system. | **Not validated** and should not be marketed as achieved. | Product design and a contract cannot establish clinical/financial causality or legal compliance. |

The public reviews are useful clues for interview language and workflow observation. They are not testimonials for MediMind. The founder-authored [Medplum case study](https://www.medplum.com/blog/medimind-case-study) supports product direction and a reported anchor contract, not verified improvement for every hospital or a license to claim a full network go-live.

## Step 2 — Build the full problem stack

These are **hypotheses for three buying contexts**, not fictional customer quotes. “Future” describes a possible consequence over 6–12 months if the pattern continues, not a forecast. In every row, another cause or a narrower fix may prove more important than replacing hospital software.

| Buyer context | Surface problem they could name | Underlying problem to test | Hidden problem to test | Possible 6–12-month future if unresolved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Independent private-hospital CEO or owner** | “The same patient journey creates repeated handoffs, calls, and entries” is proposed wording, **not a quote**. | Clinical, scheduling, billing, and reporting steps may lack a shared, trustworthy handoff, or the workflow may be poorly designed even when the system is integrated. | The leader may fear spending scarce resources on software while staff remain frustrated, or may fear a failed replacement more than present inefficiency. | The same rework and delayed information may keep consuming staff capacity. The size of any financial or clinical effect needs measurement. |
| **Hospital-network CEO or group operator** | Site reports and patient pathways may be hard to compare or standardize across hospitals. | Different sites may use inconsistent definitions, contracts, systems, and practices. A shared database alone cannot resolve governance. | The leader may fear that growth is outrunning oversight and that a central change will alienate local clinical teams. | Expansion or improvement projects may require repeated reconciliation and site-by-site customization, if current remedies remain inadequate. |
| **Clinician-owner or medically led hospital leader** | Staff may spend attention navigating records, repeating documentation, or chasing results instead of patient work. | The information needed for a decision may be scattered, poorly presented, or delayed by local process. | The leader may fear that an AI or software initiative will erode clinical trust, obscure responsibility, or make care feel less personal. | Staff frustration and workarounds may persist; potential safety effects require clinical review, not a marketing inference. |

Committee viewpoints sharpen the same stack rather than multiplying invented personas. Finance asks whether a charge was actually collected and whether total cost improved. Operations asks who does the extra work. Clinical leaders ask whether information is accurate and whether safeguards remain. IT asks who owns interfaces, access, backup, and exit. A purchase requires these jobs to align.

## Step 3 — Identify the problem worth leading with

**Lead with a measurable question:** **Why does one patient create so much extra work across your hospital?** This is a proposed campaign hook, not a buyer quotation or proof that every hospital has the problem. It is specific enough to bring clinical and business work into one conversation and to start with a recent, observable pathway. It is broad enough for a CEO to invite the right committee members, while the first demo can narrow it to one task.

Its evidence status is **partially supported externally**: several first-person EHR users describe repeated charting or task navigation, and other users praise the benefits of connected records [P6]. Internal buyer notes independently name cross-department friction [I1]. What remains unproven is local urgency, economic weight, replace-versus-fix preference, and MediMind's actual net improvement. The honest message is an invitation to **map and measure the hospital's own pathway**, not a statement that MediMind has already eliminated the work.

For the first discovery call, ask for the last real patient journey that required duplicate entry, a call to another department, a manual spreadsheet, or bill reconciliation. If the buyer cannot name one with a consequence and owner, do not force this hook onto the account. If the incumbent can fix it satisfactorily, the operating-system sale may not be justified.

## Step 4 — Write three problem statement versions

**Rational, for an analytical decision-maker:** “When a patient moves through clinical, scheduling, and financial steps, staff may have to re-enter or reconcile the same information. Before replacing anything, count the handoffs, minutes, errors, and actual payment effects in one pathway; then compare the current remedy with a shared workflow.” This is data-grounded in **what must be measured**, not a fabricated quantified loss.

**Emotional, for a story or interview invitation:** “Your people know how to care for a patient. It is exhausting when they also have to carry the patient's information from screen to screen and chase the answer another team already has.” This is proposed language to test with staff and leaders, not an attributed quotation.

**Identity, for a CEO who sees themself as a responsible improver:** “You should be able to show your team a better way to run one patient pathway—and prove that it helps—before asking them to trust a hospital-wide change.” This frames the leader's responsibility without accusing them of neglect or promising zero transition risk.

The three versions share one underlying job: make a specific improvement visible and defensible before a larger commitment. The emotional and identity versions are hypotheses about what will resonate; do not label them validated by review-site users.

## Step 5 — Problems I should not claim to solve

1. **“We guarantee better clinical outcomes or detect deterioration before clinicians do.”** Shared patient context and AI assistance can support review, but autonomous hospital-wide clinical decisions and attributable patient outcomes have not been established. Clinical decisions remain under qualified human control. Any safety claim needs the exact use case, validation, and approved workflow.
2. **“We recover all lost revenue or fix hospital finances.”** Payer rates, contracts, clinical coding accuracy, staffing, and management choices may explain much of a financial gap. MediMind may help show documentation or process issues in an agreed workflow, but only actual legitimate collections and net costs can establish a financial result.
3. **“We replace every system in two weeks, at the same total cost, with no disruption.”** The founder's two-week parallel-evaluation target is readiness dependent; it is separate from full production migration and clinical sign-off. Matched current software price does not establish migration, support, interfaces, optional AI, or lifetime terms. A hospital OS still interfaces with outside systems and devices.

These boundaries also protect the product from drawing a buyer whose only priority is a narrow ambient scribe, a one-off dashboard, or a compliance guarantee that no supplier can make without a defined legal and operational scope.

## Step 6 — Problem-solution fit statement for each type

The required format is used as a **hypothesis**. “Solves” means the proposed mechanism for a scoped pathway, not a measured universal outcome; each statement must be updated after interviews and evaluation.

**Independent private-hospital CEO/owner:** “The hospital leader struggles with repeat work and delayed answers along a patient pathway because information and responsibilities can break at clinical-to-business handoffs. Unlike another unmeasured change request to the current supplier, MediMind proposes to connect the selected steps on a shared hospital record and compare a tailored workflow beside current operations, so the leader can decide whether the total work improves enough to justify a wider change.”

**Hospital-network CEO/group operator:** “The group leader struggles to compare and improve the same pathway across sites because systems, definitions, and practices may differ. Unlike manually stitching site reports together, MediMind proposes a shared clinical and operational foundation with agreed local adaptations, so the group can test more consistent information and spread a verified improvement site by site.”

**Clinician-owner/medically led leader:** “The clinical leader struggles when staff chase patient context or repeat documentation because the needed information is not available at the right step. Unlike adding a stand-alone tool to one screen, MediMind proposes to bring the chosen clinical and supporting business steps into one reviewable workflow, so clinicians can test whether they get the context they need with less avoidable work while retaining clinical control.”

Each statement should be rejected or rewritten if a buyer's actual root cause is staffing, policy, contract terms, or a narrow fix that works better. The next test is a real baseline and acceptance criterion, not a vote on whether this prose sounds compelling.
