# Prompt 9 — Build Your Customer Avatar

> **Status and inputs, 23 September 2026.** This is a **synthetic working avatar**, built for message and interview design. It is not a portrait of a named real buyer, a composite from completed interviews, or a prediction that all Georgian CEOs behave alike. No target-buyer interview findings exist yet. Inputs are the Prompt 1 market, Prompt 4 working JTBD/UVPs, Prompt 5 alternatives, Prompt 6 staff-review language, Prompt 7 unexecuted research plan, and Prompt 8 problem stack. If only one buyer context could be served first, choose the CEO/owner or group operator of a Georgian private multi-profile hospital who can name a recurring cross-department problem and assign a clinical/operations owner plus an IT counterpart to an evaluation. The exact sentence this buyer says aloud is **unknown**; the proposed signal in the reference card must be tested.

## Step 1 — Build the full eight-dimension avatar profile

**Avatar label: The accountable hospital operator.** This is a role description, not a fictitious person's identity. Details of calendar, fears, and media habits are hypotheses to check. The day-in-life scene below is illustrative, with no implied patient, hospital, or interview case.

| Dimension | Working portrait and what would verify it |
|---|---|
| **1. Who they are** | A CEO, owner, or group operator responsible for clinical continuity, finances, staff capacity, and major supplier decisions in a Georgian private hospital or network. They may work in Tbilisi or a regional center; the location, age, gender, family situation, and personal life are intentionally unspecified. Their day could move between a management report, a department concern, an operating decision, and a supplier discussion. Confirm actual decision remit and daily schedule in interviews. |
| **2. What they are trying to achieve** | Over the next **6–12 months**, identify an important hospital-workflow problem, improve it without disrupting care, and show leadership and staff a result they can inspect. This is a planning horizon, not a reported personal goal. Define success with the buyer: e.g., reliable handoff information, fewer repeated steps, faster report preparation, or appropriately measured actual collections after total costs. |
| **3. What stands in their way** | External: old contracts, interfaces, inconsistent records, limited staff time, training load, supplier dependencies, and clinical/IT approval. Internal: rational aversion to a failed change; possible belief that another dashboard or coordinator is safer. These barriers require case-specific proof, not a generic “resistance to change” label. |
| **4. What they have already tried** | Possible paths to ask about: renewing the current system; requesting a vendor change; adding spreadsheet checks or coordinators; buying reporting/integration or a focused AI tool; considering a broader EHR; postponing. **None is asserted as this avatar's history.** The research interview should establish which path was actually tried and whether it worked. |
| **5. How they make decisions** | Likely a mix of operational evidence and trust. They may ask finance for a real business case, clinical leaders for patient-safety and usability review, IT for security/integration/exit requirements, and staff for workflow acceptance. Purchasing authority, contract calendar, and formal procurement steps vary by organization; map them rather than presuming a simple CEO signature. |
| **6. What they fear** | Rational: downtime, missing data, unplanned total cost, a young supplier that cannot support the hospital, and an AI mistake that blurs clinical accountability. Possible emotional fear: being remembered as the leader who distracted staff with a costly modernization that did not improve work. Interviews must establish whether these fears are present and ranked. |
| **7. What they secretly want** | A defensible, tangible improvement that staff notice and owners can trust; freedom to improve care and operations without negotiating every small change from scratch. This is creative direction, **not a discovered confession**. A buyer may instead value stability above visible innovation. |
| **8. A day in their life** | Illustrative scene: The leader starts with a site-performance check. Before a management meeting, an operations lead reports that one patient pathway required phone calls and corrections between registration, a clinical team, and billing. Finance can show a delay but cannot yet say whether money was lost. The CEO asks what the incumbent can change, what staff effort the workaround costs, and whether a limited comparison would be safe. Later, clinical and IT leaders raise data and cutover questions. The exact scenario is a research prompt, not a reported event. |

The most useful research correction would be a buyer who says their most urgent issue is not cross-department repeat work, or who explains that current software is excellent and staffing or payer rules are the actual cause. The avatar should then change.

## Step 2 — Build the avatar's emotional landscape

1. **Dominant emotion — cautious frustration (hypothesis).** If a recurring pathway absorbs skilled staff time, the leader may be frustrated by continued rework while remaining wary of a risky core change. Neither emotion has been directly measured in Georgian buyers.
2. **Aspiration emotion — earned confidence.** They want to see evidence that a change makes work better and that staff can still care safely. Confidence comes from a transparent comparison, not a promise that there is no risk.
3. **Social context — the committee is watching.** Owners or a board may ask about cost; clinicians about safety and work; finance about collections; IT about control and continuity. A careful first improvement could strengthen the leader's credibility. This social interpretation needs interviews, not storytelling alone.
4. **Relationship with solutions — informed skepticism.** They have likely seen software promises, but the specific experience is unknown. A narrow tool or incumbent change may be an intelligent choice. MediMind earns attention by understanding the hospital's actual pathway, showing boundaries, and stating what is and is not accepted or measured.

## Step 3 — Write the avatar in their own voice

**Clearly synthetic monologue (about 190 words).** This is original creative writing. It is **not** a verbatim buyer quote and should never be attributed to a Georgian hospital leader. One short phrase about click burden is borrowed from a cited Epic staff review [R1] as a language cue, not evidence that a CEO said it. Real buyer language should replace this after interviews.

> I can see how hard our people work. What I cannot always see is why a single patient creates so many separate jobs for them. Someone checks a record, someone calls another team, and someone later has to make the numbers agree. I need to know whether that is an occasional exception or a pattern worth changing. I also need to know which part is software and which part is our process.
>
> I have no interest in putting care at risk for a prettier screen. Our current system may be frustrating in places, but staff know how to use it. A nurse saying there are “too many clicks” matters to me if it points to real time lost or missed information. I want my clinical and IT leads to inspect the same pathway with me and tell me what would make a test safe.
>
> Show me one improvement that stands up to comparison. Show me the work my team would have to do, the full cost, and the rule for stopping. If we can prove that first step, I can talk about a larger change with confidence.

The monologue avoids a fabricated patient incident, CEO quotation, recovery amount, or personal biography. The quoted three-word phrase is from [Tabatha W., supervising nurse, 9 April 2026](https://www.capterra.com/p/122278/EpicCare-EMR/reviews/) and is the only reused review wording here.

## Step 5 — Identify the avatar's buying trigger

**Working trigger hypothesis:** A recent management or clinical-review meeting exposes a repeated patient-pathway handoff that staff have been compensating for, and the current supplier's answer does not provide a credible fix within the leader's operating horizon. A software contract renewal, new site, incident, or difficult monthly reconciliation could bring that moment forward. There is **no evidence that this is the single most common trigger** among Georgian buyers; Prompt 7 must test it.

At that moment the leader may feel urgency mixed with caution: frustration about the recurring work and anxiety about disrupting the hospital. The first likely action is to ask the workflow owner and incumbent supplier for a concrete account of what happened and what can be changed. That action is also unverified, so MediMind should be visible with a helpful pathway-mapping guide and a founder-led 25-minute demo invitation, then ask for a real example. Do not insert a fake crisis or an artificial deadline.

An operational qualification sequence follows: Is there a recent case? Is its consequence meaningful? Is an owner assigned? Has the narrower remedy been considered? Can clinical and IT counterparts set safe evaluation conditions? A “yes” to an AI headline alone is not the trigger.

## Step 6 — Build the avatar reference card

| Field | Working answer for the team |
|---|---|
| **Name / description** | **The accountable hospital operator:** CEO, owner, or group leader of a Georgian private multi-profile hospital who can sponsor a first cross-department pathway evaluation. A role label, not a real person. |
| **Primary 6–12-month goal** | Show one meaningful care/operations improvement with a defensible measure, then decide whether wider system change is justified. Hypothesis. |
| **Biggest obstacle** | The known system and workarounds may be inadequate, but a core replacement brings data, staff, continuity, and supplier risk. |
| **Dominant → aspiration emotion** | Cautious frustration → earned confidence. Hypotheses to test. |
| **Signal sentence** | Proposed buyer-language test: “Why does one patient create so much extra work?” **Not an actual CEO quote.** Ask interviewees for their own sentence. |
| **Top three suggested watering holes** | Georgian Healthcare Association provider network/news; relevant LinkedIn professional connections; permissioned peer introductions. Actual attendance/usage unknown. |
| **Buying trigger** | A recent cross-department problem plus an unsatisfactory current remedy, often surfaced in a leadership review. Frequency unverified. |
| **Say to earn trust** | “Let's map one pathway, the work and cost it creates, and the conditions for a safe comparison; your clinical and IT team decide whether the result is good enough to expand.” |
| **Never say** | “We can replace everything in two weeks with no risk and guarantee better care or recovered revenue.” It confuses a scoped parallel evaluation with production acceptance and asserts unmeasured outcomes. |

Use this card to draft questions and prototype a message. Replace its speculative lines with actual words, decision steps, and channels from at least four independent hospital organizations before treating it as an evidence-based persona.
