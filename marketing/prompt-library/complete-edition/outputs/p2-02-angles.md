# Part 2, Prompt 2 — Define Your Marketing Angles

> **Status and required inputs (23 September 2026).** Prompt 1's funnel is a founder-led hybrid, with a 25-minute pathway demo and a proposed measured evaluation as the principal transition from interest to a decision. The synthetic avatar is the accountable Georgian private-hospital operator; Prompt 8's lead question is “Why does one patient create so much extra work?” The top three working UVPs are: find and test repeat work, compare beside the current system before switching, and give teams a clearer patient story. The BFG matrix documents 25 hospital domains and two cross-cutting capabilities but no independently measured outcome. Competition includes the incumbent, narrow tools, broad vendors, and workarounds; the small public-review corpus is mostly non-Georgian staff and cannot supply a CEO quote. No target-buyer interview language has been collected. This document uses **proposed buyer language**, not an attributed customer's exact words.

**Four current-situation answers.** The leading fear to test is that a core switch disrupts care or revenue and leaves the leader responsible for an expensive mistake. What the buyer most wants to be able to say is, “Our team tested one important pathway, saw the real work and cost, and made a careful decision.” This is synthetic language, not a quotation. A live site and founder-authored material exist, but no verified marketing traffic, lead, or channel-performance series was supplied; the demo form's source-observed simulated submission needs a live check and repair before promotion. The founder did not provide brands they admire. As editorial references only, borrow HubSpot's useful diagnostic asset, Salesforce's committee-aware enterprise education, and Monday.com's easily understood visual demonstration; none establishes a suitable acquisition benchmark for Georgian hospitals.

## Part 1 — Master marketing angle

### 1. Hook

**Why does one patient create so much extra work across your hospital?**

The question gives a busy executive something they can test against a recent case. It points to one patient journey, not a claim that every hospital is broken. A reader who does not recognize the problem can still learn whether it exists by mapping one pathway; a reader whose problem is staffing or payer policy should not be pushed toward core replacement.

### 2. Differentiator

The template in the library demands “the only.” There is no global comparison that supports exclusive ownership of a hospital operating system, FHIR R4, AI, or parallel evaluation. The defensible one-sentence version keeps its structure without fabricating exclusivity:

**Unlike adding another point solution to a repeated handoff, MediMind is a hospital operating system that lets a private hospital map and compare a tailored patient pathway across clinical and business work before deciding on a wider switch.**

The difference to demonstrate is the combination of shared scope, site-specific path, founder development relationship, and controlled comparison. It is a testable positioning claim, not a declaration that no competitor could offer any of those elements.

### 3. Primary psychological trigger

**Commitment and consistency** is the most useful initial trigger: ask the leader to name a recurring problem they already agree should be fixed, then make the next action a small, consistent step—map one pathway. This avoids asking them to endorse a wholesale replacement before they have evidence. Loss aversion is present in the fear of failed transition, but the campaign should answer it with control and proof so the desired emotion becomes earned confidence from a hospital-owned comparison.

**Priority 3** is the demo invitation framed as a controlled parallel comparison: “See your pathway beside today's work before deciding.” The first three active angles therefore form one story: **map the problem → show a patient-pathway use case → compare safely**.

## Part 6 — 30-day angle execution plan

Five hours below is the **total marketing allocation each week** across this prompt, the funnel plan, and campaign work. The binding weekly clock reserves **85 minutes** for routine work: five five-minute weekday checks (25 minutes total), a 30-minute Monday scorecard review, and a 30-minute amortized monthly cleanup reserve. That leaves **215 minutes (3 hours 35 minutes)** for the creative and outreach actions in each row. The founder's 25-minute sales meetings are additional sales time; reliable form submission and analytics setup require engineering time. Do not launch traffic to a simulated request route. Week 1 contains a route verification gate, while drafting and direct discovery can continue independently.

| Week and leading angle | Create and publish within five marketing hours | Measure | If the previous week misses its signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 (days 1–7): low-risk pathway CTA** | **215 min active:** 75 min build T10 one-page worksheet; 40 min write/publish T01 post; 60 min send five individually relevant, permissioned notes; 40 min respond and ask for the last handoff. Publish only when P01 works. **85 min routine** as above. | Relevant replies, completed maps, and accepted route submissions—not impressions. | If no relevant reply, ask two known operators which wording describes a real handoff; revise T01 rather than making more formats. |
| **2 (days 8–14): use-case offer** | **215 min active:** 85 min film T04 phone video with a synthetic pathway; 40 min caption/edit; 10 min publish with T10 link; 80 min follow up with interested leaders. **85 min routine.** | A hospital leader names a pathway or asks for a demo. | If week 1 had no signal, use a simpler recent incident question, test it in direct conversation, and publish only the version a buyer recognizes. |
| **3 (days 15–21): controlled comparison** | **215 min active:** 85 min make M10 readiness checklist; 40 min write M02 post; 30 min publish/share with prior responders; 60 min answer readiness questions. **85 min routine.** | Named clinical/operations and IT counterparts, evaluation interest, held qualified demo. | If week 2 gets attention but no next step, show what the buyer leaves with after 25 minutes and check the actual request path. |
| **4 (days 22–28): combine and learn** | **215 min active:** 85 min create B04 synthetic demo preview; 15 min publish with verified P05 link; 75 min personally follow up; 40 min inspect the last four account journeys and choose one correction. **85 min routine.** | Cumulative held qualified demos versus the 12/90-day scenario, plus evaluation readiness. | If week 3 yields demos without evaluations, ask exactly which prerequisite is missing; change the offer or qualification, not the volume of content. |

Days 29–30 use the routine checks and next Monday review from the following weekly allocation; they add no fifth creative block to the 30-day plan. There is no paid budget and no fabricated webinar or event date. The first month succeeds when the system can receive a real inquiry, the founder can hold a qualified pathway demo, and a hospital can clearly accept or decline an evaluation. A small sample cannot validate one hook over another; use buyer explanation and the next step they take.
