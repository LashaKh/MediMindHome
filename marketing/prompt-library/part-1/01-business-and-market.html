Strategy brief / 01   ·   Business & market

A better hospital today.
A credible path to its AI future.

Your customer wants a better-running hospital—not another software purchase. Solving today’s problems makes MediMind worth buying. The AI future makes the opportunity bigger.

Audience Hospital CEOs & decision-makersFocus Georgia first · provisionalStatus Working thesis
01Restate the business sharply

One system. A more capable hospital.

MediMind replaces disconnected hospital software with one tailored system that connects care and business operations, helps teams reduce wasted work and missed revenue, and creates a foundation for hospital-wide AI.

The hospital is the customer. Its CEO, owner, or finance leader owns the spending decision. Clinical and IT leaders help determine whether the change is safe, useful, and feasible.

01 / Today

Connect the work.

Bring information and workflows together. Reduce avoidable coordination and make losses and bottlenecks easier to see.

02 / Keep improving

Build around needs.

Add useful AI and continuing improvements around the hospital’s real priorities.

03 / The destination

Enable proactive AI.

Build toward coordination across clinical and business work, with appropriate human control.

The bridge to buying: let the hospital evaluate agreed workflows before replacing its current system.

This is the intended value story, not proof that all stages are live. Replacement scope is agreed per hospital; external portals, devices, and services may still require integration.

PDedicated buyer pain points / added 23 September

What the hospital CEO needs to fix.

Money that is hard to account for. Staff time consumed by repeat work. Decisions made without a clear picture. Care that depends on people connecting the dots.

The central buying problem: improve how the hospital runs without putting care, revenue, or daily operations at risk during the change.

Working priorities—not a measured ranking of Georgian buyers. The six pains below come from our saved buyer research and the complete library’s problem analysis. No new buyer interviews were conducted. Different hospitals may rank them differently.

01 / Money & margins

Care delivered. Payment delayed or missed.

Documentation, charges, payment records, and actual costs do not always line up. Finance spends time finding gaps and correcting records.

Why the CEO cares: uncertain collections and avoidable costs leave less room to invest in care.

What would prove it?Actual payment corrections, collection delays, and comparable case costs—not just charges identified.

02 / Staff capacity

Skilled people doing the same work twice.

Teams re-enter information, search across screens, call other departments, and check what someone else already knows.

Why the CEO cares: scarce staff attention goes into keeping work moving instead of caring for patients.

What would prove it?Observed repeat entries, minutes per task, overtime, and total workload before and after.

03 / Leadership control

A hospital picture that arrives too late.

Departments report different numbers. Leadership waits for spreadsheets and reconciliation before it can trust the answer.

Why the CEO cares: problems can grow before leaders see them; sites are hard to compare.

What would prove it?Time to prepare reports, disputed figures, data freshness, and decisions delayed.

04 / Patient care

Doctors chasing the patient’s story.

Relevant history, results, and handoff information are scattered or arrive late. People fill the gaps with calls and repeated checking.

Why the CEO cares: care becomes harder to coordinate, with concern about delays and missed information.

What would prove it?Information-retrieval time, review delays, and handoff completeness. Clinical outcomes need separate evaluation.

05 / Ability to improve

Every improvement becomes another wait.

Change requests bring new quotes, uncertain timelines, or suppliers saying another system is responsible.

Why the CEO cares: improvement plans stall while workarounds multiply.

What would prove it?Real change requests, delivery times, accepted changes, backlog behavior, and total costs.

06 / Reporting & accountability

Rebuilding the record when someone asks.

Staff manually reconstruct what happened for management, payment, or external review.

Why the CEO cares: reporting consumes attention and creates uncertainty about whether records are complete and consistent.

What would prove it?A real reporting task, preparation effort, missing records, and traceability checks—not a blanket compliance claim.

Two fears can stop the purchase—even when the pain is real.

“Will changing make things worse?”

Missing history, training, extra parallel work, downtime, and payment disruption can outweigh the expected benefit.

Our response: agree on scope, effort, success criteria, and a controlled switch decision with clinical and IT owners.

“Can one young supplier support all this?”

One system reduces vendor coordination but concentrates dependency. Support, continuity, data access, and exit arrangements matter.

Our response: make responsibilities, service expectations, development terms, and data access explicit.

The two questions are illustrative buyer-language hypotheses, not customer quotations. AI readiness is a strategic opportunity to test, not yet a proven leading buying pain.

02Name the real market

The buyer is choosing whether to change.

Start with private hospitals and groups whose current systems make financial control, staff productivity, or care coordination difficult—and whose leadership is ready to act.

What they can do insteadWhy it makes senseWhat we must establish
Keep their supplierFamiliar tools, relationships, and routines.The needed improvement cannot arrive at acceptable speed, cost, or quality.
Use people and spreadsheetsStaff understand exceptions and can bridge the gaps.The recurring work has a material cost or operational consequence.
Add a focused toolSolve one urgent problem without replacing everything.A narrow fix leaves important cross-department problems unresolved.
Choose another hospital systemEstablished references, broad scope, or a preferred implementation partner.Our actual proposal offers a better fit and acceptable transition.
WaitAvoid the immediate risk and effort of change.The ongoing problem matters more than the comfort of postponing.
Qualify by complexity + pain + authority + readiness.
Bed count alone does not tell us whether a hospital needs MediMind or can buy it.

Recommended focus: Georgian organizations with a feasible evaluation path. Regional expansion is the next hypothesis; the international vision is not today’s qualified pipeline.

03Surface the biggest assumptions

Three things we need to prove.

These are not reasons to lower the ambition. They are the evidence needed to make it convincing.

A.

The pain justifies replacement.

A CEO can dislike the current software without being willing to change it. Frustration does not automatically create a buying decision.

Best testAsk about a recent costly incident, attempted fixes, and willingness to assign people and time to an evaluation.

B.

The improvement is measurable.

Connected information must improve real work—not simply make the interface look better. Software is not the only driver of results.

Best testMeasure one agreed workflow before and after, including transition effort and any added work elsewhere.

C.

The offer is repeatable.

Matched pricing, broad scope, rapid delivery, and continuing development must remain workable beyond the first partner.

Best testTrack delivery hours, support costs, reusable work, and acceptance at a second independent hospital.

Keep the evidence precise. Time saved is not automatically cash saved. Better processes are not automatically proven patient outcomes. An anchor relationship is not proof of broad demand.

“I can fix what is holding my hospital back, have a team that keeps improving the system, and prepare for AI—after seeing whether it works for us.”

Illustrative buyer thought—not a customer quote. The proposed combination is promising, not proven globally unique.

The hard question: if the hospital can solve its important problem faster and more safely with a smaller change, why replace the whole core system? Answer with the hospital’s actual work and evidence—not architecture alone.
07Write the market positioning

The recommended strategic foundation.

Positioning statement / working draft

MediMind is the hospital operating system for private hospitals and hospital groups ready to move beyond disconnected software. We bring clinical and business work into one system, tailored to the hospital and built to reduce repeated work, reveal avoidable losses, and support better-informed care. Our approach combines continued development with a parallel evaluation, so the hospital can judge the change before switching. We start with the problems leadership needs to solve today and build the connected foundation for progressively more capable AI across the hospital.

A strategy paragraph—not the final homepage headline or a guarantee of outcomes.

Commercial offer / terms to settle

Make the opportunity accessible.

Proposed wording: “For eligible early partners, we match current software pricing and include ongoing development within an agreed scope.”

The requested unlimited-development offer still needs an operating definition. This is a proposal for review, not a change to the live offer or a permanent price lock.

Urgency / grounded in the problem

The cost of waiting is today’s friction.

Every month spent compensating for disconnected work is effort the hospital cannot use to improve care and operations.

A scoped evaluation can establish whether there is a better option. Use genuine early-partner terms; do not invent a deadline or predict that hospitals will disappear.

Before public use: verify deployment scope, clinical and financial evidence, delivery timelines, and commercial commitments. Keep available capabilities separate from the proactive-AI roadmap.

02 / Dedicated public pain section

Your people should care for patients.
Not hold disconnected software together.

When information stops at each department, the work does not. Your teams fill the gaps—with repeated entries, phone calls, spreadsheets, and billing corrections.

  • Staff time goes into repeat work.
  • Payment gaps and avoidable costs become harder to see.
  • Care teams have to chase the information they need.

Adding another tool does not always fix what happens between them.

03 / The replacement

One hospital.
One connected way to work.

MediMind brings clinical and business work into one tailored hospital system. Keep patient information connected, make daily work easier to follow, and give leadership a clearer view of how the hospital is performing.

04 / Continuing development

Your hospital keeps evolving.
Your software should too.

Work directly with the team building your system. Shape improvements around your hospital’s needs, test changes with your staff, and keep making the system better.

05 / The AI destination

The future is a hospital that can anticipate.
Not just react.

Start with connected information and AI that assists daily work. Our roadmap builds toward a hospital-specific AI system that helps anticipate risks and coordinate clinical and business workflows—with your clinical team retaining control of medical decisions.

06 / Evaluation

See the difference.
Then decide to switch.

Start with an agreed workflow. Compare a tailored MediMind setup beside your current system. Review the results, staff effort, and transition plan with your team before deciding on a wider rollout.

Why this is the recommended version.

  • It keeps the full promise: a hospital-wide replacement, ongoing development, and an AI future.
