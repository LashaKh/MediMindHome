# What hospital decision-makers need to fix

Date: 22 September 2026
Status: Provisional research synthesis, not a validated ranking or approved public copy.
Working audience: CEOs, owners, and finance leaders of private hospital chains and mid-to-large multi-profile hospitals, initially in Georgia. Clinical and IT leaders influence and approve the decision.

Source IDs below resolve in the [evidence register](2026-09-22-evidence-register.md). Internal positioning is evidence of what MediMind believes, not proof of what every buyer believes. External research provides context, not local customer validation.

## The central finding

Our strongest current hypothesis is that the buyer needs **a hospital that performs better without putting care, finances, or operations at risk during the change**.

Disconnected software is a possible cause. A hospital operating system is our mechanism. AI is part of the approach and future opportunity. The buying reasons are more concrete: money lost, staff time consumed, decisions delayed, care harder to coordinate, and improvements blocked.

Your own August buyer notes explicitly put reimbursement gaps, avoidable costs, and operational visibility ahead of the software bill [I2]. That makes “same software price” a useful objection remover, not necessarily the headline benefit.

The current hero expresses a desirable destination. It does not yet show the CEO that we understand the specific problem they have to explain at their next management meeting.

## Priority pains to investigate

This is a research priority order based on internal specificity, likely buying relevance, and plausible product fit. It is not a survey-derived order of all hospital problems. A clinical safety event or an audit deadline may immediately change the order for a particular hospital.

All first-person sentences below are **proposed buyer-language hypotheses, not quotations from customers**.

### 1. The hospital does the work but loses money along the way

**Proposed buyer language:** We treat the patients, but we lose money in the paperwork and cannot see where costs are getting away from us.

- **Visible problem:** Care documentation, charges, payer submissions, and actual costs do not line up reliably. People spend time correcting records and chasing payment.
- **CEO consequence:** Weaker cash collection, uncertain profitability, and less room to invest in care. Distinguish cash delayed from revenue permanently lost.
- **Potential software-related causes:** Missing or inconsistent documentation, manual handoffs, missed legitimate charges, and poor visibility into resource use. Payment rates, payer decisions, contracts, and clinical complexity may be bigger causes that software cannot fix.
- **Trigger to test:** Repeated rejected claims, a weak financial month, or an unexpected difference in costs between similar services/sites.
- **Existing workaround to investigate:** Manual billing checks, spreadsheets, extra finance staff, documentation reminders, or an additional finance tool.
- **MediMind fit:** Connect clinical work and financial records; make missing information and spending patterns easier to inspect. Specific workflows and results need demonstration.
- **Proof needed:** Documented reason codes for rejected claims; attributable corrections; money actually collected; cost per comparable case; added implementation/support cost.
- **Evidence:** Specific internal economic-buyer thesis [I2]. U.S. CEO research supports the broader financial pressure [E1]; it does not establish Georgian losses or MediMind's effect.
- **Status:** Partially supported context; customer-specific cause, amount, and willingness to replace remain unvalidated.

### 2. Expensive, scarce staff spend too much time on avoidable work

**Proposed buyer language:** I need my team caring for patients, not entering the same information again and again.

- **Visible problem:** Repeated entry, searching across screens, phone calls for results, and rechecking what another department already knows.
- **CEO consequence:** Paid time diverted from care, slower service, staff frustration, and possible overtime. Extra hiring does not fix the underlying work design.
- **Trigger to test:** Overtime growth, clinician complaints, vacancies, or a service backlog.
- **Existing workaround:** More coordinators, paper lists, messages, manual reconciliation, and informal staff knowledge.
- **MediMind fit:** Shared information, simpler workflows, and selected AI assistance. We need to show less work, not merely fewer applications.
- **Proof needed:** Observed minutes per task, repeat entries, completion/error rates, training burden, and user adoption before/after.
- **Evidence:** Internal coordinator/clinician pain statements [I1]; workforce concerns in U.S. CEO research [E1]; the Georgian health-system review reports an acute nursing shortage [E2]. None proves that software causes the whole staffing problem.
- **Status:** Partially supported; local workflow burden and reducible fraction need measurement.

### 3. Leadership cannot get a timely, trusted picture of the hospital

**Proposed buyer language:** By the time I get a report I can trust, the problem has already cost us.

- **Visible problem:** Different departments report different numbers; the management team waits for people to compile and reconcile information.
- **CEO consequence:** Decisions about capacity, spending, staffing, and performance rely on incomplete or late information. Chain leaders cannot compare hospitals confidently.
- **Trigger to test:** A board meeting with disputed numbers, an unexpected overspend, or a new hospital joining the group.
- **Existing workaround:** Spreadsheet consolidation, more meetings, and calls to department heads.
- **MediMind fit:** A shared operational record and consistent reporting definitions. A dashboard alone does not fix bad data or unclear accountability.
- **Proof needed:** Report preparation time, data freshness, reconciliation errors, and a real decision improved by the information.
- **Evidence:** Automated unified visibility is named in the internal economic-buyer notes [I2].
- **Status:** Internally supported hypothesis; no direct executive quote or observed reporting cycle reviewed.

### 4. Care depends on people manually connecting the patient story

**Proposed buyer language:** My doctors should not have to chase information to understand the patient in front of them.

- **Visible problem:** Relevant history, current care, results, and handoffs are scattered or arrive late.
- **CEO consequence:** Delays, repeated work, difficult coordination, and concern about missed information. Clinical leaders may rank this above money.
- **Trigger to test:** A documented near miss, a delayed decision, repeated tests, or complaints about handoffs.
- **Existing workaround:** Phone calls, paper summaries, repeated checking, and staff carrying knowledge between departments.
- **MediMind fit:** Bring relevant patient context and workflows together. This supports better coordination; it is not proof of fewer deaths, complications, or readmissions.
- **Proof needed:** Time to retrieve relevant information, result-to-review delay, handoff completeness, appropriate safety review, and eventually properly designed outcome evaluation.
- **Evidence:** Internal clinical workflow thesis [I1]. U.S. interoperability research shows existing data exchange is common but uneven in method and coverage [E3]; it does not prove an individual hospital has this pain.
- **Status:** Plausible and internally supported; severity and clinical improvement not established locally.

### 5. The hospital cannot improve at the speed its needs change

**Proposed buyer language:** Every change becomes another quote, another wait, or another vendor saying it is not their responsibility.

- **Visible problem:** Small changes take too long, customization costs are hard to predict, and different vendors own different pieces.
- **CEO consequence:** Improvement plans stall; staff build workarounds; leadership pays repeatedly without getting a coordinated result.
- **Trigger to test:** An expensive change request, a failed integration, contract renewal, or a workflow the current vendor cannot support.
- **Existing workaround:** Accept the limitation, hire developers, buy another tool, or rely on spreadsheets.
- **MediMind fit:** A coordinated product and continuing development relationship. Unlimited requests are not the same as infinite delivery capacity or instantaneous production changes.
- **Proof needed:** Actual request-to-test times, acceptance rates, backlog behavior, support response, and total change costs.
- **Evidence:** Internal vendor and integration pain [I1]; founder's development offer [I4].
- **Status:** Internal hypothesis with a proposed commercial response; no comparative service-performance dataset.

### 6. Reporting and audit readiness consume work and create exposure

**Proposed buyer language:** When someone asks us to prove what happened, my team has to reconstruct it.

- **Visible problem:** Records need manual preparation for management, payment, or external review; different sources may not agree.
- **CEO consequence:** Staff burden and uncertainty about whether the hospital can demonstrate its processes and records reliably.
- **Trigger to test:** An audit, rejected reporting, a new contractual requirement, or a security incident.
- **Existing workaround:** Manual exports, additional checks, outside assistance, and last-minute reconciliation.
- **MediMind fit:** Consistent data, traceable workflows, and reporting support where implemented and verified. Software does not guarantee legal compliance or security.
- **Proof needed:** A real required report, audit-log completeness, access review, restoration tests, and buyer security review.
- **Evidence:** Internal CIO/ministry reporting concerns [I1]. This is not an assertion about a particular law or a certification MediMind holds.
- **Status:** Internal hypothesis; rank and requirements vary by jurisdiction and institution.

## Two buying objections that can outweigh every benefit

### A. Can we change without disrupting care or revenue?

The buyer can dislike the current software and still rationally keep it. Migration, missing history, staff training, parallel work, downtime, and a failed cutover can outweigh the expected gain.

The parallel-evaluation offer addresses this objection only if it has a limited scope, authorized data access, named hospital owners, an honest workload estimate, reconciliation, success criteria, and a controlled decision to switch [I3]. “Nothing changes until you agree” is more defensible than “zero effort” or “no risk.”

**Evidence to request:** What caused the last replacement proposal to stall? Who vetoed it? What was the specific feared loss? What would make a limited evaluation acceptable?

### B. Can we trust one young supplier with so much of the hospital?

Replacing multiple vendors reduces coordination burden but concentrates dependency. The CEO and CIO may reasonably ask about long-term support, continuity, data access/export, service levels, security, and whether the promised development capacity is sustainable.

These are buying requirements, not objections to dismiss. Involve IT as an evaluation partner. Do not carry forward the older internal framing of IT as merely a blocker to neutralize.

**Evidence to request:** What vendor failure would be unacceptable? What support and exit provisions are mandatory? Which promises in our offer cause doubt?

Both objections are strongly relevant to the proposed sales motion, but their ranking is a hypothesis until buyers confirm it.

## Where the AI future belongs

**Proposed buyer language:** I want useful AI, but I need to know it works here, is safe, and will not become another disconnected expense.

AI can be an aspiration, a board priority, or an active buying trigger. We have not established which it is for the primary buyer. Ask for a specific AI project, assigned budget, accountable owner, decision deadline, and reason it is stuck.

Position the future as an extension of useful progress: connected workflows first; AI assistance where it works; broader proactive coordination as capabilities are developed and validated. Do not imply full replacement is the only route to any AI use. Do not claim autonomous clinical decisions are already deployed, inevitable hospital failure, or a future malpractice deadline.

The urgency should come from a verified ongoing problem or a genuinely bounded commercial offer. A fear of being left behind is not yet a proven purchasing motive.

## Problem layers by buying situation

The hidden and future layers are hypotheses to test, not psychological facts about buyers. Future consequences are plausible, not forecasts.

| Buying situation | Surface problem | Possible underlying cause | Hidden concern to test | If unchanged over 6-12 months |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CEO/owner under financial pressure | Weak margins and difficulty tracking avoidable loss | Care, billing, and cost information do not connect reliably | Being accountable for results without enough control; making an expensive wrong move | The same documentation and coordination problems may keep consuming cash and time |
| Chain leader seeking consistent performance | Hospitals cannot be compared or managed consistently | Separate systems, definitions, local workflows, and reporting | Expansion may increase complexity faster than oversight | Each new site may add another layer of manual coordination |
| Clinically led hospital | Staff chase information and work around software | Fragmented patient context and poorly supported handoffs | Modernization could make care harder before it makes it better | Workarounds become entrenched; improvement projects remain delayed |

The finance, clinical, and IT participants have different acceptance criteria within each buying situation. They are not necessarily separate customer markets.

## The problem worth leading with, provisionally

**Costly coordination failures that consume staff time, obscure financial losses, and make good care harder to deliver.**

This ties the operating problem to the CEO's responsibilities without assuming every financial or clinical problem is caused by software. The supporting reason to engage is a controlled way to verify improvement before switching.

Three exploratory statements, not approved landing-page copy:

- **Rational:** Disconnected systems make your hospital repeat work, reconcile records, and struggle to see where money is being lost.
- **Emotional:** Your people work hard to keep the hospital running. They should not have to keep its software working together, too.
- **Leadership:** Your hospital's progress should not depend on how long its software vendors take to catch up.

Recommended message order to test: recognizable present-day problem, concrete improvement, evidence, manageable evaluation, then the larger AI opportunity. The current same-price and development offer should support that story rather than substitute for it.

## Provisional problem-solution fit

- **Independent CEO/owner:** When care, financial records, and daily work are disconnected, leadership struggles to identify and reduce avoidable loss. MediMind's proposed unified workflows offer a way to connect them; a scoped evaluation must demonstrate better work and economics before replacement.
- **Hospital-chain operator:** When sites use inconsistent records and processes, central leadership struggles to compare performance and spread improvements. Shared workflows and reporting could reduce repeated coordination, while preserving necessary local differences; this needs multi-site evidence.
- **Clinically led hospital:** When patient context is scattered, staff spend effort reconstructing it. Connected records and workflow support could reduce that effort and improve handoffs; clinician testing and measured safety review must establish the actual benefit.

## Boundaries: what not to claim

- Do not promise that software solves inadequate reimbursement rates, staffing supply, poor management, or every source of hospital loss.
- Do not promise dramatic or immediate clinical/financial results without attributable evidence.
- Do not describe a prototype or parallel trial as a validated clinical release or completed migration.
- Do not claim every system, external portal, or specialized device can be replaced without a scope review.
- Do not imply advanced AI is included forever at matched pricing until the commercial conflict is resolved.

## What would change this ranking?

Promote a pain when several independent target hospitals describe a recent incident without prompting, can show its consequence, have tried to solve it, and assign an owner or budget. Downgrade it when leaders merely agree that it sounds important but cannot name an incident or take a next step.

A single anchor customer's enthusiasm is valuable but cannot establish market-wide priority. Keep financial-loss, staff-workload, and leadership-visibility messages separate in early tests so we can learn which actually drives interest.
