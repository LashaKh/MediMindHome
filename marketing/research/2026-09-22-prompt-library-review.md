## The useful change in approach

The strongest lesson is to start with the buyer's concrete problem, its consequence, and the reason they act. A bigger promise about AI cannot substitute for evidence of what the buyer wants to fix. The next creative round should be a response to that evidence.

## What to use from Library #0

- Demo prompt, pp. 3-7: identify the top three pains, previous attempts, fear of engaging, and a measurable desired result. Explain what the buyer will actually see and leave with.
- Webinar prompt, pp. 8-13: use only if we choose a specific educational event. It is not the immediate missing piece.
- Trial prompt, pp. 14-20: borrow clear evaluation outcomes, security review, setup effort, and exit conditions. Do not transplant a self-serve signup-in-minutes model into hospital-wide implementation.

For MediMind, a tailored parallel evaluation is different from a generic free SaaS trial. Its prerequisites, permitted data, scope, staff workload, success criteria, and exit process matter more than a credit-card reassurance.

## Where the libraries need judgment

1. **They cannot supply missing research.** Prompt 6 allows extraction from model knowledge, then asks for exact customer words. We require inspectable sources for quotes; otherwise wording is a hypothesis.
2. **A memory command is not stored evidence.** It cannot establish that earlier answers exist. Reusable Markdown files and source links provide continuity.
3. **Their conversion benchmarks are not our forecasts.** Claims such as 120% improvement from shorter forms, 84-270% from nearby social proof, or a universal retention benchmark are not sourced in these documents. Do not use them as MediMind targets or guaranteed effects.
4. **A score is not validation.** A model's 5/5 desirability score does not show a hospital will buy. A real problem, recent action, accountable owner, and willingness to run a scoped evaluation are stronger signals.
5. **One buyer profile cannot represent every role and market.** The CEO authorizes the investment; finance tests economics; clinical leadership tests care/workload; IT tests safety, support, and feasibility.
6. **The hospital buyer may be right not to switch.** Replacement is one option, not a universally necessary precondition for AI. Existing systems can integrate with other tools. We need to demonstrate why replacement is better in the target hospital's situation.
7. **Do not force an unsupported uniqueness claim.** The suggested “only” positioning format requires competitive evidence. A specific, defensible offer is preferable to invented exclusivity.
8. **Do not borrow competitor reviews as MediMind success stories.** A complaint can inform research; it cannot become a testimonial or proof of our results.

## Five assumptions to test first

1. Operational losses and coordination problems are painful enough to justify replacing core software, rather than adding reporting or fixing one workflow.
2. CEOs rank the economic/operational problem above AI ambition when committing budget, staff, and risk.
3. A parallel evaluation can produce trustworthy evidence without an unacceptable extra burden on clinical and IT teams.
4. Matched pricing and continuing development feel credible and financially sustainable, rather than too good to be true.
5. Shared hospital data and workflows can deliver a measurable improvement in the selected use case, not merely a cleaner interface.
